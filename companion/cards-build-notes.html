The card build: revision notes

FICTITIOUS DATA Scope note. Real: the build itself, every structural claim made about it, and every design change described below. Invented: the studies and the people the changes answer to — the card sort, the tree test, their participants, and the whole Timpanogos Shadows Ward roster — which are the companion’s standing fiction. What’s real and what’s not →

A prototype is not a draft that becomes a final; it is a sequence of bets, and each revision should show its reasoning. This page is the reasoning for the third build of the first functional prototype: the listings redesigned into skill cards, on a simpler, whiter shell. Each change below is tagged with where it came from — evidence when a study finding or a named ward member demanded it, conviction when it is design judgment standing on its own. Both are legitimate. Confusing them is not.

One prototype, three builds

The graded chain freezes a prototype the moment its study runs. It does not freeze the instructor — so v1 has grown a line of revisions, each one building on the last, each one a candidate for what the final prototype should absorb. Watch what changes and what refuses to change.

  • v1

    Team A’s turn-in. The tree test’s six decisions built exactly as written: one data file, two hierarchies plus search, a hand-built 35-concept search vocabulary, the click-and-query log. The build the task test will run on. Built November 2026 (course fiction); the binding artifact.

  • v2

    The semantic build. One subsystem replaced: search grown into a precomputed meaning index, so “kids are noisy” lands without appearing in any skill’s wording. No server, no AI call at lookup time. Reported and graded, 57/60 → Instructor revision.

  • v3

    The card build — this revision. The semantic build’s data and engine, untouched and loaded by reference; every listing redesigned from a title row into an informative card, and the visual shell pared down. Instructor revision, August 2026 (authoring time).

The problem v3 answers

Through two builds, a listing told a teacher almost nothing. A row carried the skill’s manual sentence (or, for five lucky blocks, a tree-test situation label), the verbatim wording underneath, and moment tags — and the manual’s sentences are teacher-development prose, not Saturday-night prose: “Create student self-evaluations about a doctrine, truth, or principle” is accurate and inert. A teacher scanning eight of those rows is doing translation work at exactly the moment they have the least patience for it. The card sort told us this in so many words: Cami Sprague (P15) flagged 7 of 24 card faces as vocabulary she could not act on (“Is search question a church word or a real word?”), and our answer then — harvested language at the category and group levels — fixed the navigation without ever fixing the listings. The rows a teacher actually chooses between were still written in the manual’s voice.

So v3 redesigns the row into a card that answers, in order, the three questions a teacher brings: is this my situation? — what is it, exactly? — what would I actually do?

The card, element by element

1 · A situation lead on all 53 blocks evidence + conviction

Every card now leads with the situation the skill is for, in teacher words: “When a hand goes halfway up and comes back down.” This extends the tree test’s D4 decision — which gave five blocks a harvested situation label and measurably helped scanning — to the whole inventory. The five D4 labels were justified from participant wording and lead their cards verbatim, untouched. The other 48 are written from my own ear for how these teachers talk, which makes them the same kind of content as the semantic build’s situation sentences: load-bearing, plausible, and not evidence. The semantic build’s grading priced exactly this move at two points, and the price is accepted here knowingly — the task test can now check whether situation leads help or mislead, because every participant will see them on every list.

2 · The manual’s own sentence, always second, always present evidence

The verbatim skill sentence sits directly under the lead in smaller type, clamped to two lines on a card (the block page always carries it in full). It never disappears: the card sort’s settled rule was harvested labels at the levels people navigate, verbatim manual language one level down — the situation lead gets you to the card, the manual’s sentence tells you exactly what you found, and the pairing is what keeps plain language from becoming paraphrase drift.

3 · The first move evidence + conviction

Each card carries one imperative sentence: the smallest concrete thing a teacher could do with the skill. (“Text one parent tonight: ‘Here’s what I noticed about your kid on Sunday.’”) Two people on the roster demanded this element. Margie Ostler (P01) — eleven ten-year-olds, preparing at the kitchen counter Saturday night — is not short of content and said so: she is looking for something to do. Devin Ashworth (P17) reads a manual “the way he reads an install manual: straight through, once, looking for the step he is supposed to do.” A card that ends without a step fails both of them. The 53 sentences themselves are authored, with the same caveat as change 1. The block page carries the same line in its own panel, so the promise the card makes is kept where it lands.

4 · A prep chip: “no prep — use it live” vs “takes getting ready” evidence

Every card says whether the skill is usable in the moment with empty hands, or requires doing something before Sunday. The attribute is derived, not invented: it reads off the manual’s own verb for the skill — create, prepare, plan, study, send mean “ahead”; ask, listen, respond, invite, testify mean “live.” The reason it earns card space is the roster’s time budget: Whitney Kartchner (P04) prepares one-handed on her phone at 11 p.m. after three twelve-hour shifts — a skill that needs materials gathered is, for her, a skill that does not exist. Sione Latu (P03) prepares in his truck between jobs. A teacher like either of them can now narrow a result list to what is still possible tonight — the search grew a matching “narrow by preparation” facet alongside category, group, and moment.

5 · The matched-reason line, clamped to one line evidence

The semantic build’s why-lines (“your ‘phones’ is close in meaning to…”) kept their honesty and lost their sprawl. The grading of that build docked its visual score one point for precisely this: on a phone the why-line was “the longest element in the card, set in the smallest type,” and the eye landed mid-quote instead of on the title. Truncate it, or reveal it on demand, the grader wrote. v3 truncates: one line, ellipsis, full stop. This is the cleanest kind of revision — a finding from the previous cycle, applied as written.

6 · Moment chips stay; the card is built to be scanned on a phone evidence

The lesson-moment tags keep their place in the card footer — the tree test’s moment-view entries succeeded at 95.5% against 72.7% for category entries (22 runs against 88, so we hold the claim loosely, but it has pointed one direction twice now). And the card’s proportions are set for a phone held in one hand, because that is where this ward actually prepares: Margie’s phone is propped against the toaster, Whitney’s is over her head in bed, Sione’s is in a cup holder, and Sydnee Hafen (P20) “will have found the page while somebody older is still reading the first screen.” On a narrow screen the cards run single-column, the verbatim sentence keeps its two-line clamp, and nothing in the card requires a hover.

7 · The simpler shell conviction

The visual system is Team A’s “Sunday Blue” — same tokens, same one-accent-per-screen discipline, same four rules for where the accent lands — but the page spends less of it. The pale blue wash and the floating white surface are gone; content sits on white. Borders dropped to their lightest step, corners rounded, chips lost their outlines, the header is a plain bar. No study told us to do this, and I will not pretend one did: it is judgment that a card-based screen carries more internal structure than a list-based one, so the frame around it should say less. If the task test finds people missing the edges of things, this is the first change to revisit.

8 · What the search does with the new words conviction

The situation leads join the literal search layer at weight 3 — above the branch names, below the manual sentence, and half the weight of the five committed D4 labels — because authored language must never outrank the studies’ own words. The meaning index, the committed 35-term vocabulary, and the ranking arithmetic are inherited from v2 unchanged.

What this revision refuses to touch

The information architecture is inherited by reference — the build loads Team A’s data file, so all six tree-test decisions, the trial “What it feels like” branch, and every filing and moment are byte-for-byte the turn-in’s own. The trial branch in particular stays exactly as the tree test left it: its label is the next study’s question, and dressing it up now would contaminate the answer. The log schema is unchanged across all three builds, each under its own storage key, so a task test can run any build — or any two, between subjects — without contaminated data. And the accent discipline survives a third build: the cards added structure without spending color.

What I am ignoring

The authored layer keeps growing. 48 situation leads and 53 first moves are conviction wearing the studies’ clothes — they sit in the exact seat the harvested D4 labels sit in, and a participant cannot tell which is which. That is the point (the interface should not read like a bibliography), and the risk: if one of my situation leads names the wrong situation, it will confidently route people to the wrong skill. The task test is the check, and its task set should route through at least one authored lead on purpose.

Cards cost vertical space. A 13-block group that was one screen of rows is now two-plus screens of cards. The bet is that richer scanning beats shorter scrolling for this corpus at these list sizes; the wrong side of that bet looks like people giving up mid-list, and the click logs will show it.

The prep chip is binary. “Takes getting ready” covers both “pick a hymn” and “run a written self-evaluation over a week.” A finer scale was drafted and cut — three uncertain values are worse than two honest ones. If the chip earns its place in the task test, the final prototype can afford the finer question.
