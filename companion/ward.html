Meet the Timpanogos Shadows Ward

Every study in this companion needs participants, and the participants have to be the same people from study to study — same calling, same phone, same worries — or the arc stops reading as one project. So we built a ward. This is the reference sheet the studies draw from: when a study says “P04,” it means the person listed under P04 here, and it will keep meaning her.

The ward is described as it stands in November 2026 — about ten weeks into the Church’s new Sunday meeting schedule. That timing puts every teacher here in the middle of relearning a job they had already spent twenty years learning, which is the most interesting moment there is to study anybody.

Scope note

FICTITIOUS DATA

Everything on this page is invented — the ward, its numbers, all twenty people, and every word in quotation marks. None of these people are based on real individuals, and nothing here was observed, surveyed, or counted. What is real is the shape: a North Orem neighborhood where households stay twenty to forty years, where the teachers are diligent and untrained and short on time, and where nobody has ever said “skill development” out loud. The site the studies examine is real and deployed, and so are the design decisions the studies land on.

Speculation note: this page guesses about the future

SPECULATION Written August 2026 · set November 2026

The schedule change is real. In March 2026 the First Presidency announced a new Sunday schedule, effective September 2026: sacrament meeting up to sixty minutes, then twenty-five minutes of Sunday School every week, then twenty-five minutes of elders quorum, Relief Society, Young Women, or an Aaronic Priesthood quorum — also every week — with five-minute transitions. Primary runs fifty-five minutes alongside. Quorums and Relief Society study recent conference messages; the youth use the updated For the Strength of Youth guide; teacher councils become monthly, held during a class period. The guidance asks for lessons built around one to three key principles, preferring meaningful discussion over covering all the material.

How this ward experienced it is not real, and could not be. This page was written in August 2026, before the new schedule had been used for a single Sunday by anyone, anywhere — and it describes a ward ten weeks in. Every account of the change’s reception is therefore a guess about a future nobody had lived, stamped SPECULATION. Read those passages as design fiction, not prediction.

Ward geography and rhythm

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented place, invented detail

The ward sits on the bench in North Orem, six streets wide, on old orchard ground platted into quarter-acre lots in the 1970s. Half the ward can walk to church; the other half drives four blocks because of the hill. The meetinghouse is shared with two other wards; Timpanogos Shadows meets at 11:00 a.m.

Since the first Sunday in September the block has had the new shape: for everyone from twelve up, nothing alternates any more, nothing skips, and no one has a week off. Primary runs fifty-five minutes alongside — opening, singing time, then class — which is exactly what Primary looked like in August and a decade ago. The thirteen Primary workers are the only people in the building whose Sunday did not move an inch, and for ten weeks they have been watching everyone else relearn a job they themselves were never asked to put down.

SPECULATION Guessed reception, written in advance

Ten weeks in, the hallway has not read the instruction to socialize before and after church rather than between meetings: the conversations that used to fill a leisurely gap now run at the same length inside a five-minute one, so classes start with a trickle — and three minutes late to a twenty-five-minute class is an eighth of it gone. The reframe is landing better than the timing: fellowship in the foyer is no longer loitering, it is the instruction, and this ward is good at exactly that.

Who lives here

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented population

The single most important fact about this ward is that people do not leave it. Households stay twenty to forty years, so the ward has two ends and a thin middle: a large, rooted population of empty-nesters and grandparents, a small growing band of newlyweds in the turned-over houses and basement apartments, and a forty-to-fifty-five band that is the smallest group in the ward — which is exactly why those few households carry the heaviest callings. Occupations run the way North Orem runs: BYU and UVU faculty and staff, tech commuters, nurses and PAs, small business owners, and a long bench of retired people who used to be all of those things.

The teaching corps

Twenty-six adults are called to stand up and teach something on a Sunday: 13 in Primary, 4 in Sunday School, 4 with the youth, 3 in elders quorum, 2 in Relief Society.

Primary is the biggest teaching operation in the building: twelve class teachers across seven classes plus the music leader, most of them either grandmothers with thirty years of practice or young mothers called four months ago. It is also the one organization the September change did not touch at all — same order, same length, same Sunday. Sunday School has four teachers for four classes, now weekly: fifty-two lessons a year instead of twenty-four, each half as long. The arithmetic says total teaching time barely moved; the teachers will tell you the something else is preparation, not minutes. Young Women and the quorums meet every week in the second period, four advisers working from the youth guide’s monthly themes. SPECULATION Elders quorum keeps three instructors against a weekly meeting, so a turn comes every third week instead of every sixth. Relief Society runs leaner: two instructors alternating weekly — a doubling, and the hardest number on this page to live with. The two have been talking since October about asking for a third; as of November neither has raised it.

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented corps profile

  • Church experience: deep.

    Twenty-three of twenty-six born in the church; eighteen served missions; eight have held stake-level or bishopric callings. They know how a meeting works and how to show up.

  • Training in teaching: essentially none.

    Not one has been trained to teach a gospel lesson. The closest is Sister Tolman (P13), who taught fifth grade for twenty-eight years — and she is the first to say almost none of it transfers. Everyone else runs on instinct and memory of their own teachers.

  • Devices: the phone is the tool.

    Twenty-two of twenty-six prepare primarily on a phone, standing up, in fragments — Saturday evening or Sunday morning, in twenty-minute pieces, interrupted.

  • They mean it — and they all worry about the same thing.

    Nobody is coasting; what they are short on is time and training, not care. And when the Sunday School president asked what they wanted help with, almost every answer was some version of getting through it: the lesson is too long, the clock too short, material left on the page at the end. Coverage is not the point of teaching — but it is what they measure themselves against.

Ten weeks in

SPECULATION What the new schedule did to the thirteen non-Primary teachers between September and November — guessed before the first Sunday of the new schedule had happened. The thirteen in Primary are not in this list, because nothing in their Sunday changed.

  • The twenty-five-minute wall.

    Subtract stragglers, prayer, and settling, and a teacher has eighteen or twenty minutes. Habits, materials, and twenty years of pacing instinct are all still sized for fifty — and the best veteran material, the stories with real setup, no longer fits. Silence got expensive too: a ninety-second pause is now six percent of the lesson, so teachers frightened of dead air have started lecturing, the precise opposite of what the short block was meant to produce.

  • Weekly cadence means weekly preparation.

    The most-felt cost in this ward. Per lesson, preparation is down; in total and in mental load it is sharply up, forever, for a corps whose weeks were already out of slack. And the conference-talk format swapped the demand: content knowledge, which this corps has, matters less; facilitation of a fresh text, which nobody was trained in, is now the whole job.

  • Coverage anxiety survived its own repeal.

    The guidance now says in print that one to three key principles beat covering the material. These teachers have measured themselves by coverage for twenty years, and being told they were measuring the wrong thing has not, in ten weeks, stopped them measuring it. For the ones who have absorbed it, though, it is the most liberating sentence the institution ever handed them.

  • What got better.

    Twenty-five minutes fits an actual attention span — teachers who spent years failing to hold a room for fifty are discovering they can hold it for twenty-five. Weekly continuity means threads carry and relationships compound. And the monthly teacher council, held inside a class period, is the first regular venue this ward has ever had for teachers to talk about teaching.

  • The rest of the building moved into Primary’s world.

    A short lesson inside a longer weekly block is the only thing Primary has ever done. The youth and adult teachers have spent ten weeks arriving, one at a time, at a format thirteen of their colleagues have worked in all along. Whether anyone thinks to go ask them about it is a question this companion finds very interesting.

The seven the studies lean on

P01

Margie Ostler

Primary · 10–11s

61 · in the ward 30 years · office manager, family dental practice (part-time) · iPhone, three years old, cracked corner

Thirty years in the same house, seven grandchildren, one of them in her own class — the ten-and-elevens, eleven of them, the biggest class in Primary, which she got because she is the person the president calls when a class is falling apart. She prepares standing at the kitchen counter Saturday night, phone against the toaster, and she is looking for one thing: something to do with eleven kids that will hold them. Nothing about her Sunday changed in September, and she has found the autumn faintly surreal — her problem in November was her problem in 2011: the children arrive from singing time already up, already loud, and she spends the first three minutes getting them back down.

“I don’t need more ideas. I need eleven kids in their chairs at the same time for ninety seconds.”

Trent Larkin

Sunday School · adult class

Four kids, two of them teenagers, one car in the shop at any given moment. He reads the guidance about key principles every week. He believes it is true. He teaches as though it is not.

P03

Sione Latu

Elders quorum · instructor

52 · in the ward 19 years · owns a small remodeling business · older iPhone, text size cranked up, Gospel Library and nothing else

Mission, former bishopric counselor, more church service than almost anyone in the corps. P04

Whitney Kartchner

Young Women · 14–15s

33 · in the ward 4 years · nurse practitioner, three twelve-hour shifts a week · iPhone, one-handed, at 11 p.m.

Two children under five, three long shifts a week, everything else in the gaps. P05

Curtis Openshaw

Sunday School · 16–18s

71 · in the ward 38 years · retired university facilities supervisor · iPad his daughter set up; prints the outline at the library

The ward’s long-tenure archetype: same house since 1988, former ward clerk, former high councilor. Six years with the oldest Sunday School class, and very good at the part of teaching that cannot be taught — the kids know he likes them, and they come. A sixteen-year-old convert (P09) joined his class in June, and Curtis has begun noticing how much of what he says only works if you already know what it means — not defensive about it, a little shaken. The schedule has been harder on him than anyone: his printed outlines were built for a fifty-minute room, and what he cuts first is the stories, which take setup — and which are the reason those kids come. He knows both things at once and has not found the third thing that resolves them.

“I can get to the point in twenty-five minutes. It’s the getting there that I was ever any good at.”

P06

Jenae Rowley

Relief Society · instructor

38 · in the ward 6 years · grant administrator, university research office · MacBook and iPhone; the only person here who makes an outline document

The most organized person in the teaching corps and would be embarrassed to hear it said. Three children, a traveling husband, a job with deadlines. She and Aubrey (P12) are the entire Relief Society rotation, so she now teaches every other week — double what it was — and she is the only one who does the whole job on a laptop, reading the source end to end and building an outline with headings. How this ward talks

Ward speech, recorded as raw material for the studies — the words these people use in the hallway when a lesson has gone sideways.

FICTITIOUS DATA These phrases are invented for CS 356. Nobody said them. They are written to be the kind of thing this kind of teacher plausibly says — which is what makes them useful, and what would make them dangerous if mistaken for evidence.

What they say

  • “My class won’t settle.”
  • “We had no reverence at all today.”
  • “I ran out of time again.”
  • “I didn’t get through the lesson.”
  • “There’s so much material.”
  • “Nobody answers. They just sit there.”
  • “It’s a hard lesson this week.”
  • “I need something to do with my hands.”
  • “How do I make it apply to them?”
  • “Somebody asked me a question I couldn’t answer.”
  • “Do you have any ideas for this one?”
  • “What did you do for that lesson?”
  • “I’m not a teacher. I’m just the one who got called.”
  • “My kids were wild.” (“my kids” means the class)

What they never say

  • “skill development”
  • “learning objective”
  • “engagement strategy”
  • “facilitation”
  • “scaffolding”
  • “assessment”
  • “differentiation”
  • “pedagogy”
  • Any name of a principle category, ever, unprompted
  • The number of a lesson, without looking it up

Two words carry almost the whole load instead: challenge and problem. “My challenge is getting them to talk.” “The problem is the last ten minutes.”

SPECULATION New since September

What they have started saying since the change

  • “Twenty-five minutes.” (said flatly, as a complete sentence)
  • “We barely got started.”
  • “I lost five minutes to the hallway.”
  • “The transition killed me.”
  • “Which week am I on?”
  • “Did you get to the talk, or just the first story?”
  • “It’s my week again already.”
  • “I only did one thing.” (said as an apology; the guidance would call it success)

Every one is a Sunday School, quorum, Relief Society, or youth teacher talking. Nobody in Primary says any of them, because nothing in Primary changed — its own finding, and a fairly loud one if anybody were listening for it. And note what did not change anywhere: they still say “challenge” and “problem,” still describe trouble as a story about last Sunday, and not one of them has adopted a single word from the guidance that caused all this.

A few more habits of speech

  • “The lesson” has not shrunk with the clock.

    It still means the whole class period — and the printed outline, and sometimes the topic. Which one is meant is always clear from context to them and never to anyone else.

  • “The manual” and “the app.”

    “The manual” is whatever outline they should be following; “the app” is Gospel Library. Almost nobody distinguishes the app from the church website — both are just “where it is.”

  • Trouble is an event, not a category.

    Nobody says “I have a classroom management issue.” They say “the Whipple twins were under the table” — a story about last Sunday, in the past tense, with names in it.

  • Help-seeking is peer-to-peer and verbal.

    When a teacher here is stuck, the first move is not a search box; it is asking whoever taught the same class last year, in the hall, on the way to the car. And shared vocabulary is assumed universally — the working language is dense with terms nobody defines, which works perfectly right up until there is one person in the room it does not work for.

How the studies use this page

  • IDs are permanent.

    A study cites participants as P01…P20. Those IDs are never reassigned, and a person’s calling, device, and personality stay fixed across the whole arc. If a study needs someone new, the roster grows off the end; nothing above it moves. P17–P20 arrived exactly that way, for the tree test.

  • Light entries can be promoted, never renumbered.

    The thirteen sketched people can be written out in full when a study needs them. That fills in detail; it does not change who they are.

  • The vocabulary section is source material, not findings.

    It records how these teachers talk. What a study does with that — grouping it, naming categories, testing the names — belongs to the study, not to this page.

  • This page invents people. It does not invent the site.

    Everything the studies examine — the pages, the structure, the search, the commits — is real and open in another tab.
