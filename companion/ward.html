Meet the Timpanogos Shadows Ward

Every study in this companion needs participants, and the participants have to be the same people from study to study — same calling, same phone, same worries — or the arc stops reading as one project. So we built a ward. This page is that ward: where it sits, who lives in it, who teaches in it, and the exact words its teachers use when something in a lesson goes wrong.

It is the reference sheet the studies draw from. When a study says “P04,” it means the person listed under P04 here, and it will keep meaning her.

The ward is described as it stands in November 2026 — about ten weeks into the Church’s new Sunday meeting schedule, with October general conference just behind it. That timing is not incidental. It puts every teacher in this ward in the middle of relearning a job they had already spent twenty years learning, which is the most interesting moment there is to study anybody.

Scope note

FICTITIOUS DATA

Everything on this page is invented. The ward, its boundaries, its meetinghouse, its numbers, all sixteen people, their callings, their phones, and every word in quotation marks. The Timpanogos Shadows Ward does not exist and never did. None of these people are based on real individuals, and nothing here was observed, surveyed, or counted.

What is real is the shape. A North Orem neighborhood where households stay twenty to forty years, where the teachers are diligent and untrained and short on time, and where nobody has ever said the phrase “skill development” out loud — that pattern is realistic, and it is the reason this fiction is worth writing down. The site the studies examine is real and deployed, and so are the design decisions the studies land on.

Speculation note: this page guesses about the future

SPECULATION Written August 2026 · set November 2026

There is a second layer of unreality on this page, and it is easy to miss because it sits on top of a fact.

The schedule change is real. In March 2026 the First Presidency announced a new Sunday meeting schedule, effective the first regular Sunday in September 2026: sacrament meeting of up to sixty minutes, a five-minute transition, twenty-five minutes of Sunday School every week for adults and youth, another five-minute transition, and twenty-five minutes of elders quorum, Relief Society, Young Women, or an Aaronic Priesthood quorum — also every week. Primary runs fifty-five minutes alongside. Elders quorum and Relief Society study recent general conference messages; the youth use the updated For the Strength of Youth: A Guide for Making Choices. Teacher council meetings become monthly, held during one of the class periods. The guidance asks leaders to end on time, keep transitions brief, socialize before and after church rather than between meetings, and build lessons around one to three key principles — explicitly preferring meaningful discussion over covering all the material. All of that is real, and is published by the Church on its Sunday meeting schedule feature page.

How this ward experienced it is not real, and could not be. This page was written in August 2026 — before the new schedule had been used for a single Sunday, by anyone, anywhere. It then describes a fictitious ward ten weeks into it. So every account here of how the change landed is a guess about a future nobody had lived yet: the Primary rotation that got tried and abandoned, the strain on a two-person Relief Society rotation, who found the shorter class a relief and who quietly grieved it, and every word any of these people say about any of it.

That makes two layers of invention stacked on one real event: the ordinary fiction of this companion (the ward and its people do not exist) plus the speculative fiction of their reception (the event is real; their experience of it is imagined in advance). Passages carrying the second layer are stamped SPECULATION.

Read them as design fiction, not prediction. Nothing here was forecast by anyone with data, and a real September would have produced surprises no one sitting in August could invent — which is, conveniently, the same lesson the rest of this companion is trying to teach.

Ward geography and rhythm

Invented, but built to the proportions of the place it is standing in for.

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented place, invented detail

The ward sits on the bench in North Orem, six streets wide, on ground that tilts up toward the mountain the ward is named after. It was orchard before it was anything: apple and cherry, torn out in stages between 1968 and 1984 and platted into quarter-acre lots of split-levels and ramblers. A few of the original trees are still standing in back yards, kept by people who remember what they were part of.

An irrigation ditch runs the length of the ward behind the back fences, with a gravel walking path beside it. At dusk the path is the busiest place in the boundaries. Half the ward can walk to church; the other half drives four blocks and parks anyway, because of the hill.

The meetinghouse is shared with two other wards. Timpanogos Shadows meets at 11:00 a.m., which means the building is already warm and slightly loud when they get there, and the parking lot is a negotiation.

Since the first Sunday in September the shape of that block has been the new one: sacrament meeting of up to an hour, a five-minute transition, twenty-five minutes of Sunday School, another five-minute transition, and twenty-five minutes in elders quorum, Relief Society, Young Women, or an Aaronic Priesthood quorum. Everything meets every week now. Nothing alternates, nothing skips, and no one has a week off. Primary runs fifty-five minutes alongside the two class periods — five minutes of opening, twenty of singing time, a five-minute transition, then twenty-five minutes of class. The Primary teachers used to be the only adults in the building who were on every Sunday. Now everybody is.

The rest of the rhythm is the ordinary one, and survived the change untouched. Youth activities Wednesday night. Break-the-fast at somebody’s house on the first Sunday. The Primary program in the fall, which the whole ward attends and the Primary presidency dreads and loves. A ward temple night that about eleven people make. And funerals: the Relief Society has served lunch in that cultural hall for three generations of the same families, and knows which ones want the rolls warm.

What ten weeks did to the hallway

The building was designed for a ward that moved twice on a Sunday. It now moves three times, and two of those moves are budgeted at five minutes each. Two hundred people, one main corridor, and a foyer that has been the ward’s living room for forty years.

The instruction to socialize before and after church rather than between meetings is clear, and ten weeks in, the hallway has not read it. The conversations that used to happen in a leisurely gap now happen in a five-minute one, at the same length, which means classes start with a trickle. Anyone three minutes late to a twenty-five-minute class has missed an eighth of it, and everyone knows this now in a way nobody had to know it before.

The reframe is landing better than the timing is. Fellowship before and after church is no longer loitering; it is the instruction. The ward is good at exactly that, and for a congregation whose warmth had nowhere official to live, being told to stand in the foyer and talk to people has been an unexpectedly easy thing to obey.

Who lives here

The single most important fact about this ward is that people do not leave it.

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented population

Households in these boundaries stay twenty to forty years. Couples buy in their early thirties, raise everybody, and then stay — the house is paid for, the yard is established, and the grandchildren are twenty minutes away. Turnover happens when someone dies or finally moves to a smaller place, and then the house is bought by a young couple who will also stay for thirty years.

The result is a ward with two ends and a thin middle: a large, deeply rooted population of empty-nesters and grandparents, and a small, growing band of newlyweds and young families in the recently-turned-over houses and the basement apartments. The forty-to-fifty-five band — the people who would normally absorb the heaviest callings — is the smallest group in the ward, which is exactly why those few households carry the heaviest callings and are the busiest people in the boundaries.

Occupations run the way North Orem runs: BYU and UVU faculty and staff, tech workers who commute south to the Silicon Slopes offices two or three days a week, nurses and PAs and a couple of physicians, small business owners — a contractor, a landscaper, a photographer — and a long bench of retired people who used to be all of those things.

The teaching corps

Twenty-six adults in this ward are called to stand up and teach something on a Sunday: 13 in Primary, 4 in Sunday School, 4 with the youth, 3 in elders quorum, 2 in Relief Society. This is who they are.

Primary is the biggest teaching operation in the building — thirteen of the twenty-six — with twelve class teachers covering seven classes from nursery up through the eleven-year-olds, plus the music leader. (The presidency runs Primary but does not teach a class, so they are not counted here.) The classes are unevenly sized: four in the nursery, and a combined ten-and-eleven class that, at eleven children, is both the oldest class and the biggest one in Primary. Primary was already on every week, which is why it is the one organization whose cadence the new schedule did not change — only its shape. Most Primary teachers here are either grandmothers with thirty years of practice or young mothers who were called four months ago, and there is very little in between.

Sunday School has four teachers for four classes — three youth classes and the adult class — and now meets every week, so each of those four teaches fifty-two times a year instead of twenty-four. Each lesson is twenty-five minutes instead of fifty. The arithmetic says their total teaching time barely moved; the teachers will tell you something else entirely, and the something else is about preparation, not minutes. The presidency also runs the teacher council meetings, which are now monthly and held during a class period rather than squeezed into whatever the calendar allowed — which, before September, was roughly twice a year.

Young Women and the quorums meet in the second class period every week, and four advisers teach between them, working from the updated For the Strength of Youth guide and its monthly themes. They are mostly in their thirties and fifties, and several of them are also the parents of the youth in the room, which they will tell you is complicated.

SPECULATION Elders quorum keeps three instructors against a meeting that now happens every week, so a turn comes around about every third week — where it used to be every sixth. The old complaint was that the gap was long enough to forget what worked last time. That complaint is gone. Relief Society runs leaner still: two instructors, alternating weekly, which means each of them teaches every other week where she used to teach once a month. That is a doubling, and it is the single hardest number on this page to live with. The two instructors have been talking since October about whether to ask for a third. As of November neither has raised it with the presidency.

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented corps profile

  • Church experience: deep.

    Twenty-three of the twenty-six were born in the church and have been active their whole lives. Eighteen served missions. Eight have been in a bishopric, on a stake high council, or in a Relief Society or Primary presidency at the stake level. They know how a meeting works, how a calling works, and how to show up.

  • Training in teaching: essentially none.

    Not one of them has been trained to teach a gospel lesson. The closest the ward comes is Sister Tolman (P13), who taught fifth grade for twenty-eight years — and she is the first to say that a Sunday School class is not a classroom and that almost none of what she learned transfers. Everyone else is running on instinct, memory of their own teachers, and whatever they can improvise.

  • Devices: the phone is the tool.

    Twenty-two of the twenty-six prepare primarily on a phone, standing up, in fragments. Seven also open a laptop at some point, though only one of those does the whole job on it. Three print things. Almost all lesson prep happens Saturday evening or Sunday morning, in twenty-minute pieces, interrupted.

  • They mean it.

    Nobody in this corps is coasting. They accepted the calling, they think about the people in the room during the week, and several of them lose sleep over it. What they are short on is time and training, not care.

  • And they are all worried about the same thing.

    When the Sunday School president asked the teachers what they wanted help with, almost every answer was some version of getting through it: the lesson is too long, the clock is too short, and there is material left on the page at the end. Coverage is not the point of teaching, and it is not the point of any lesson these people have ever been given — but it is what they measure themselves against, week after week.

Ten weeks in

What the new schedule did to this corps between September and November. Every word of it is a guess written before the first Sunday of the new schedule had happened.

SPECULATION The change is real; this reception is invented in advance. The two panels below are the substance of that guess.

SPECULATION What got harder

The cruelty of the timing is that this ward’s oldest anxiety and the Church’s newest instruction finally point the same direction — and the ward cannot quite believe it. The guidance now says, in print, that meaningful discussion matters more than covering all the material, and that a lesson should carry one to three key principles. These teachers have measured themselves by coverage for twenty years. Being told they were measuring the wrong thing has not, in ten weeks, stopped them measuring it. Several feel more coverage anxiety than before, not less: the same manual, half the runway.

  • The twenty-five-minute wall.

    Twenty-five minutes is not twenty-five minutes. Subtract the stragglers, the opening prayer, and the settling, and a teacher has eighteen or twenty minutes of actual teaching. Habits, materials, and instincts are all still sized for the old block.

  • Silence got expensive.

    A ninety-second pause after a good question used to be a technique. In a twenty-five-minute class it is six percent of the lesson, and it feels like a crisis. Teachers who are frightened of dead air have started filling it themselves — which is to say they have started lecturing, because lecturing is the one thing that reliably fits the time. That is the precise opposite of what the short block was meant to produce.

  • Weekly cadence means weekly preparation.

    This is the most-felt cost in this ward, and it is not close. Per lesson, preparation is down. In total, and in mental load, it is sharply up: a Sunday School or Relief Society teacher who used to face the class twice a month now faces it every single week, forever. For a corps made entirely of people whose weeks were already out of slack, the drumbeat is the thing.

  • Twenty years of pacing instinct, miscalibrated overnight.

    The veteran shape — a warm-up story, the discussion, a closing testimony — was built for fifty minutes and does not compress. The best material these teachers own is the long stuff: the object lesson with real setup, the story that takes four minutes before it turns. Much of it simply no longer fits, and the people who own it are quietly grieving. Nobody has said so out loud, because it would sound like complaining about the prophet.

  • The conference-talk format exposed the training gap.

    Elders quorum and Relief Society now work from recent general conference messages rather than a prepared manual chapter. That swaps the demand: content knowledge, which this corps genuinely has, matters less; facilitation of a fresh text, which nobody here was ever trained in, is now the whole job. The scaffolding that used to carry an underprepared teacher through fifty minutes is gone.

  • The Primary experiment that lasted two Sundays.

    With forty-four children, the Primary presidency was within the size where the children may be split into two groups that swap between singing time and classes. They tried it on the sixth and thirteenth of September. It doubled the transitions in a wing built for one, and it meant the music leader delivered her entire singing time twice in a row, to two different halves, every week. They reverted before the end of the month. All-together singing, then classes, exactly as written.

SPECULATION What got better

  • Twenty-five minutes fits an actual human attention span.

    Fifty minutes never really matched a fifteen-year-old, and the old Primary block never matched a ten-year-old at all. In several classes the behavior problems did not get managed — they got shorter than the lesson. Teachers who spent years failing to hold a room for the old class length are discovering they can hold it for twenty-five, which is a different sentence about themselves than the one they had been telling.

  • Permission, in print.

    For the teachers who have actually absorbed it, “one to three key principles” is the most liberating sentence the institution has ever handed them. It is the first time anyone in authority has told them in writing that not covering everything is not a failure. The ones who believe it have relaxed in a way that is visible from the back of the room.

  • Continuity, finally.

    Seeing the same class every week instead of every other week means threads carry. A question left open on the tenth is still alive on the seventeenth. Teachers who spent years reintroducing themselves every fortnight now know where they left off, and the relationships compound instead of resetting.

  • A room where teaching gets talked about.

    The monthly teacher council, held inside a class period rather than wherever the calendar could fit it, is the first regular, sanctioned venue this ward has ever had for teachers to talk to each other about teaching. Twenty-five minutes a month is not much. It is infinitely more than twice a year, and it is the change the Sunday School president would defend hardest.

The seven the studies lean on

P01

Margie Ostler

Primary · 10–11s

61 · in the ward 29 years · office manager, family dental practice (part-time) · iPhone, three years old, cracked corner

Margie and her husband bought their house on the ditch side of the ward in 1996 and have not seriously considered leaving it. Four grown children, seven grandchildren, two of them currently in Primary and one of them in her own class, which she says is either the best or the worst part of the calling depending on the Sunday. She has taught in Primary, on and off, for most of two decades, and is the person the Primary president calls when a class is falling apart — which is how she ended up with the ten-and-elevens, eleven of them, the biggest class in Primary. She prepares standing at the kitchen counter on Saturday night with the phone propped against the toaster, twenty minutes at a stretch, and she is looking for one thing: something to do with eleven kids that will hold them. She does not want more content. She has plenty of content.

The tighter class is, for her specific problem, the best thing that has happened in twenty years of Primary. Her eleven were never going to last the old block and both she and they knew it. Twenty-five minutes with a hard edge, she can do — mostly. What she did not expect was that the fix would create its own tax at the seam: the children arrive from singing time already up, already loud, already turned toward each other, and she spends the first three minutes of a twenty-five-minute class getting them back down. She has begun planning for a twenty-two-minute lesson and treating those three minutes as weather.

“I can hold eleven of them for twenty-five minutes. I just can’t have the first three.”

P02

Trent Larkin

Sunday School · adult class

44 · in the ward 11 years · software engineer, commutes to Lehi three days a week · Android phone, work laptop, everything in tabs

Trent is one of the thin-middle households, with everything that implies. Four kids, two of them teenagers, one car in the shop at any given moment. He teaches the adult Sunday School class and takes it seriously enough to read ahead on the train, which is where most of his preparation happens. His problem is not material and it is not confidence — he is comfortable in front of a room. His problem is that he builds a lesson with four scriptures, a video, and a question he genuinely likes, and then runs out of clock having used one of the four. Every time. He has started to suspect the plan is the problem, and has no idea what to do about that.

He is the peak case for what the new schedule did to a coverage-anxious teacher, and ten weeks in he has not adjusted. The class is weekly now, so the train reading is weekly too, and it has quietly eaten his commute. The block of Come, Follow Me assigned to a given Sunday did not shrink when the class did, and he cannot bring himself to cut it to one principle — that feels to him like deciding which parts of the scriptures the class does not get. So he still plans four things, still opens strong, and now stops mid-thought at twenty-five minutes instead of mid-thought at fifty. He reads the guidance about key principles every week. He believes it is true. He teaches as though it is not.

“They tell me I only need one principle. I have the whole chapter and twenty-five minutes and four people who came with questions. You pick the one.”

Invented quote Trent Larkin (P02)
P03

Sione Latu

Elders quorum · instructor

52 · in the ward 19 years · owns a small remodeling business · older iPhone, text size cranked up, Gospel Library and nothing else

Sione served a mission, was a counselor in a bishopric in his previous ward, and has done more church service than almost anyone else in the teaching corps. He is one of three elders quorum instructors against a weekly meeting, so his turn comes about every third week, and he prepares in his truck between jobs — phone in the cup holder, Gospel Library open, the talk scrolled with a thumb. He never opens a computer. He is warm and funny and the discussion in his lessons never dies, which everyone tells him is the hard part. He is not convinced. His worry is quieter than Trent’s: he could fill fifty minutes with good conversation and still drive home unsure whether anything happened to anybody.

Of the veterans, he has had the easiest ten weeks, and he is a little sheepish about it. Teaching from a conference talk given four weeks ago suits him exactly: it is concrete, it is short, everyone in the room either heard it or can hear it on the way to church, and there is no manual chapter standing between him and the men in the circle. October conference handed him three talks he actually wanted to argue with. And the old ache has an unexpected balm — quorum meets every week now, so on the two Sundays out of three when he is not teaching he is sitting there watching, which turns out to be the first sustained look he has ever had at whether anything is landing.

“Now I’m in there every week whether it’s my turn or not. Took me twenty years to find out what my class looks like when I’m not the one talking.”

P04

Whitney Kartchner

Young Women · 14–15s

33 · in the ward 4 years · nurse practitioner, three twelve-hour shifts a week · iPhone, one-handed, at 11 p.m.

Whitney and her husband bought a house on the north edge of the ward when the previous owners — forty-one years in that house — moved to a condo. Two children under five. She works three long shifts and does everything else in the gaps between them. She teaches the 14–15-year-old Young Women every week now and cares about them more than she expected to. All of her preparation is done in bed on her phone after the kids are asleep: she reads the outline, screenshots two things, and hopes. She was the most explicitly time-poor person in this roster, and she was the clearest voice for the coverage worry, because she was the one who had genuinely never once finished a lesson.

She is the ward’s early success story, and nobody is more surprised by that than she is. The new youth guide runs on monthly themes, and monthly themes are the first church material that has ever matched how her life actually works: she cannot plan a week, but she can sit down once, on a Sunday night after a shift, and rough out four weeks of one idea. November’s theme has been on her lock screen since the end of October. The weekly cadence that is crushing the Sunday School teachers costs her less than it costs them, because she is no longer starting from nothing each time — she is spending twenty-five minutes on the fourth quarter of something she already thought about. By November two other teachers have asked her, in the hallway and slightly awkwardly, how she is doing it.

“I still can’t plan a week. I can plan a month. Nobody ever gave me anything shaped like a month before.”

P05

Curtis Openshaw

Sunday School · 16–18s

71 · in the ward 38 years · retired university facilities supervisor · iPad his daughter set up; prints the outline at the library

Curtis is the ward’s long-tenure archetype: same house since 1988, former ward clerk, former high councilor, mission decades ago, wife in the Relief Society presidency. He has taught the oldest Sunday School class for six years and is very good at the part of teaching that cannot be taught — the kids know he likes them, and they come. He is comfortable to the point of automatic, which is the thing that has started to bother him. There is a sixteen-year-old in his class now (P09) who was baptized in June, and Curtis has begun noticing how much of what he says every week only works if you already know what it means. He has said “the Spirit will confirm it” in that room a thousand times and never once wondered what it sounded like from the outside. He is not defensive about this. He is a little shaken by it.

The new schedule has been harder on Curtis than on anyone else in this roster, and he is meeting it the way he meets everything, which is diligently and without complaint. His printed outlines — and he still prints them, at the library, on Saturday — were built over six years for a fifty-minute room. The first three Sundays in September he simply ran the old outline and got a third of the way through it. Since then he has been cutting, and what he cuts first is the stories, because the stories are what take setup. They are also the reason those kids come. He knows both of those things at once and has not found the third thing that resolves them. He is teaching every week now instead of twice a month, at seventy-one, and he has not missed one.

“I can get to the point in twenty-five minutes. It’s the getting there that I was ever any good at.”

Invented quote Curtis Openshaw (P05)
P06

Jenae Rowley

Relief Society · instructor

38 · in the ward 6 years · grant administrator, university research office · MacBook and iPhone; the only person here who makes an outline document

Jenae is the most organized person in the teaching corps and would be embarrassed to hear it said. Three children, a husband who travels, a job with real deadlines. She and Aubrey (P12) are the entire Relief Society rotation, alternating weekly, which means she teaches every other week — twice what it was before September. Others in this roster open a laptop at some point; she is the only one who does the entire job on it, reading the source end to end and building an outline in a document with headings. And she still ran out of time, which she found genuinely disorienting — she did everything right and the clock beat her anyway. She has started to wonder whether the outline is answering the wrong question, and that suspicion is the most useful thing anyone in this ward has said about teaching.

The doubling is breaking her method, and she knows it before she is willing to say it. A full read-and-outline every other week, on top of three children and a travelling husband and a job with deadlines, does not fit inside a life; she has done it since September by taking the hours from somewhere, and by November it is visible. The format is arguing with her too. A conference talk is twelve minutes long and already has its own structure — there is nothing to outline, only something to open up. Her instinct is to build scaffolding around it and her growing suspicion is that the scaffolding is the problem, which is the same suspicion she had before, arriving now from a direction she did not choose. She and Aubrey have been asking each other since October whether they can request a third instructor. Neither has raised it with the presidency. Both know the presidency is watching them.

“Every other week. I can do it, I just can’t do it the way I do it. And I don’t know another way yet.”

Invented quote Jenae Rowley (P06)
P07

Kade Beckstrand

Primary · 7-year-olds

29 · in the ward 3 years · parts manager at a dealership · Android phone, Gospel Library app, does not use a computer at home

Kade grew up two streets over, drifted away in his early twenties, married Rylee (P08), and came back — into his grandmother’s old house, which the family kept. They were sealed in the temple this spring with their two children, six and three, and the whole ward turned out for it. What baffles Kade is the mood of the veterans — he keeps overhearing people he respects talk about the change as a loss, and he cannot see the thing they are describing. “Everyone keeps asking how I’m adjusting. Adjusting from what? This is just how it is.”

    Since September his Sunday has more moving parts, not fewer — Sunday School and then priests quorum, both every week, two rooms and two sets of unwritten rules to read in one morning. The part that has genuinely helped him is the one nobody designed for him: the youth guide runs a single theme for a whole month, so for the first time he walks in knowing roughly what the subject will be. He knows what November is about. For everyone else that is a scheduling detail. For the one person in the room without twelve years of Primary behind him, it is the difference between arriving oriented and arriving behind.

How this ward talks

Observed ward speech, recorded here as raw material. Not a taxonomy, not an analysis — just the words these people actually use in the hallway when a lesson has gone sideways.

FICTITIOUS DATA These phrases are invented for CS 356. Nobody said them. They are written to be the kind of thing this kind of teacher plausibly says, which is exactly what makes them useful and exactly what would make them dangerous if mistaken for evidence.

What they say

  • “My class won’t settle.”
  • “We had no reverence at all today.”
  • “I ran out of time again.”
  • “I didn’t get through the lesson.”
  • “There’s so much material.”
  • “Nobody answers. They just sit there.”
  • “It’s a hard lesson this week.”
  • “I need something to do with my hands.”
  • “How do I make it apply to them?”
  • “Somebody asked me a question I couldn’t answer.”
  • “Do you have any ideas for this one?”
  • “What did you do for that lesson?”
  • “I’m not a teacher. I’m just the one who got called.”
  • “My kids were wild.” (“my kids” means the class)

What they never say

  • “skill development”
  • “learning objective”
  • “engagement strategy”
  • “facilitation”
  • “scaffolding”
  • “assessment”
  • “differentiation”
  • “pedagogy”
  • Any name of a principle category, ever, unprompted
  • The number of a lesson, without looking it up

Two words carry almost the whole load instead: challenge and problem. “My challenge is getting them to talk.” “The problem is the last ten minutes.”

SPECULATION New since September

What they have started saying since the change

Ten weeks of a new schedule produced a new dialect of the same complaint. None of the phrases above went away — the timeless ones are timeless — but these have joined them, and they are guesses about speech nobody has spoken yet.

  • “Twenty-five minutes.” (said flatly, as a complete sentence)
  • “We barely got started.”
  • “I lost five minutes to the hallway.”
  • “The transition killed me.”
  • “Which week am I on?”
  • “Did you get to the talk, or just the first story?”
  • “It’s my week again already.”
  • “I only did one thing.” (said as an apology; the guidance would call it success)

Note what did not change: they still say “challenge” and “problem,” they still describe trouble as a story about last Sunday, and not one of them has adopted a single word from the guidance that caused all this.

A few more habits of speech

  • “The lesson” has not shrunk with the clock.

    It still means the whole class period, whatever length the class is this year. It also means the printed outline, and sometimes the topic. Which one is meant is always clear from context to them and never clear to anyone else.

  • “The manual” and “the app.”

    “The manual” is whatever outline they are supposed to be following. “The app” is Gospel Library. Almost nobody in this ward distinguishes between the app and the church website; both are just “where it is.”

  • Trouble is described as an event, not a category.

    Nobody says “I have a classroom management issue.” They say “the Whipple twins were under the table.” The problem arrives as a story about last Sunday, in the past tense, with names in it.

  • Retired names never die here.

    The Primary class names were officially retired years ago and the classes now go by age, but half this ward still says “Valiant” and “Sunbeams” in the hallway, and everyone still knows what is meant. Official vocabulary and ward vocabulary are two different languages, and the ward is fluent in only one of them.

  • The help-seeking is peer-to-peer and verbal.

    When a teacher here is stuck, the first move is not a search box. It is asking whoever taught the same class last year, in the hall, on the way to the car.

  • Shared vocabulary is assumed, universally.

    Everyone in this ward has been in it for twenty years or was raised in the church somewhere else, so the working language is dense with terms nobody defines. It works perfectly — right up until there is one person in the room it does not work for.

How the studies use this page

  • IDs are permanent.

    A study cites participants as P01…P16. Those IDs will never be reassigned to a different person, and a person’s calling, device, and personality stay fixed across the whole arc. If a study needs someone new, the roster grows past P16; nothing above it moves.

  • Light entries can be promoted, never renumbered.

    The nine sketched people can be written out in full when a study needs them. That fills in detail; it does not change who they are.

  • The vocabulary section is source material, not findings.

    It records how these teachers talk. What a study does with that — grouping it, naming categories, testing the names — belongs to the study, not to this page.

  • This page invents people. It does not invent the site.

    Everything the studies examine — the pages, the structure, the search, the commits — is real and open in another tab.

  • The study pages are being revised against this setting.

    This ward moved to a November 2026 timeline after several studies were already written, so for a while you may find a study that still describes the old Sunday rhythm — alternating weeks, a fifty-minute class. Where a study and this page disagree about the schedule, this page is current. Participant identity was never affected: the IDs, names, and callings are the same ones the studies already cite.
