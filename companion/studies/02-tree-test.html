Part 1.1 — The wireframe

Link: Team A wireframe — prototypes/wireframe/team-a/. Black and white, one font, generic boxes, no images. It runs from a single data file, wireframe-data.js: one record per information block, 53 of them, each carrying a category, a group, and a moment list. No skill is written into any page. Every screen is produced by selecting on those attributes, which is why both views reach all 53 blocks without us filing anything twice.

Part 1.2 — Design rationale

The five categories are the card sort’s, not ours. “Getting people to talk,” “Knowing and loving the people you teach,” “Teaching about Jesus Christ,” and “Getting ready before Sunday” each came out of six of our eight sorts; “Keeping their attention” came out of three, and we kept it because all three of those sorters were the Primary teachers and half this ward’s teaching corps teaches children. The L1 and L2 labels are built out of words participants actually wrote on their sticky notes — “Getting them talking to each other,” “Not answering it yourself,” “Getting yourself ready” — because the newest teacher in our sort couldn’t parse 7 of the 24 official skill names. The verbatim manual wording appears only at level 3, where there is context around it, and each L3 page links out to the real manual page it came from.

There is deliberately no Spirit category. One sort in eight made one. We wrote in our card-sort report that this was our riskiest call and named the condition that would reopen it: if people look for what they do not sort by, we reopen it. That condition is a tree-test question, which is why task T09 exists.

The second view is an attribute, not a sixth box. Our sort produced one fully sequential sorter, an “Openers” pile, and a splitter pattern where the same two cards sat together in six of eight sorts while the pile’s meaning changed around them. Our read was that people are answering two questions at once — what problem does this solve and when in the lesson does it happen — so moment (the first few minutes / in the middle of the lesson / the last few minutes / after class and during the week) is a value on every block rather than a category. The by-moment view is rendered from that attribute alone. A block with two moments appears under both, and nothing about the category view changes.

The clusters our sort said never separate stayed together. The four discussion cards live in one category; the two Christ cards that co-occurred in 8 of 8 sorts share a group; praying for a student and messaging a parent are both under “Caring about them during the week.”

Logging. Every click is recorded with the page it happened on and the route it produced, and the log exports as JSON or CSV. We also built the bonus test mode: ten scenarios, order shuffled per run, timed to a tenth of a second, stored so a run survives a reload, with an “I would give up” button because a participant quitting is a result and we wanted to be able to record it. Part 2 of this report is a tree test we ran inside that mode.

T09: this is not a labeling problem

T09 is the worst result in the study and the only one where the first click does not explain the failure. 6 of 11 opened “Teaching about Jesus Christ” — the category that actually contains the answer — and only 3 of those 11 runs ended on it. 3 of 11 opened “Knowing and loving the people you teach,” and all three of those runs failed. 2 entered by moment. So the first click scattered three ways and none of the three ways was reliably wrong, which is the signature of something other than a mislabeled branch.

The rest of the numbers say the same thing louder. Three participants gave up, and all three gave up standing on the home screen after having been inside two or three categories — they did not quit lost in a subtree, they quit back at the top, having looked at our five category names again. T09 collected 19 backtracks across 7 runs; the other nine tasks collected 20 between them. Average time was 88.8 seconds against a study-wide 32.3. People searched hard, searched broadly, came home, and stopped.

Our card sort said one sorter in eight made a Spirit pile, and we concluded that a Spirit category was not what people sorted by. We still think that is what the sort showed. T09 shows the other half: it is what people look for. Those are different questions, and this is the exact condition our card-sort report named as the one that would reopen the decision.

T04: the answer depends on which door you came in

T04 succeeded 7 of 11, and the split is almost perfectly clean by entry view. All 4 participants who entered by lesson moment reached the block in two clicks — the moment page put the answer in front of them, and none of them hesitated. Of the 7 who entered by category, 3 reached it, 3 stopped on a wrong block, and 1 gave up after 7 clicks and 96.4 seconds.

The wrong blocks say what happened. 2 of the 3 misses ended on 006.title_number1, which is the skill immediately adjacent in the manual to the correct answer 006.title_number2, and which we filed under “Teaching about Jesus Christ › Finding Him in the scriptures” because it is about symbols. The correct answer is the same manual page’s other half and we filed it under “Keeping their attention › Something they do, not watch” because it is a hands-on activity. Both filings are defensible. That is the problem: 5 of 11 first-clicked “Teaching about Jesus Christ,” found a Christ-symbols skill there, and reasonably stopped. This is precisely the case our card sort predicted — a block that people reach for from two different questions — and we filed it once anyway.

T01: the first click hid the finding

T01 succeeded 10 of 11, and by pass rate it is one of our best tasks. The second click is where it goes wrong. Of the 9 participants who first clicked “Getting ready before Sunday,” 5 opened “Getting yourself ready” and found the answer; the other 4 opened “Planning the lesson” first, and all four backed out and crossed over. The five who went straight there averaged 20.1 seconds; the four who crossed over averaged 47.9 — more than twice as long, for the same task, ending in the same place.

Nobody failed, so nothing in the results table flags this. But two sibling labels that half the room cannot tell apart is a real defect, and the cost is currently being paid in seconds instead of in errors. It is also why we predicted this task wrong: we assumed people would reach for “Saturday night” as a moment and they reached for it as a phase of preparation.

The pattern across the wrong turns

Count the failures by where they stopped and the shape is consistent: 16 of the 21 non-give-up misses in the whole study ended inside the correct category, and 8 of those ended inside the correct group. Our L1 is doing its job. What is failing is the last step — distinguishing two sibling leaves, or two sibling groups, once you are already in the right neighbourhood. That is a labeling and wording problem at L2 and L3, and it is a much cheaper problem than a category problem. The one exception is T09, where the failure is at L1 and is not a wording problem at all.

The moment view, and what we are not claiming about it

22 of the 110 runs were entered through the by-moment view. Those 22 succeeded 95.5% of the time at a mean of 27.9 seconds; the 88 category-view runs succeeded 72.7% at 33.4 seconds. That looks like a large effect and we are not claiming it is one. Those 22 runs are not a random sample of runs — they are mostly one person. Nathan Kim entered by moment on all ten of his tasks and went 10 for 10; Sydnee Hafen did on six; the remaining six moment runs are spread across three people. A comparison of 22 self-selected runs against 88 is a comparison of participants, not of views, and 22 runs from effectively three or four people cannot support a claim about a population of teachers. We are recording the number and treating it as a hypothesis for v1’s logs, not as a finding.

What the moment view did demonstrably do is carry T04: every one of the 4 people who came in that way reached the answer in two clicks while the category view was splitting five ways on the same task. That claim is about one task and 4 runs, it is stated as such, and it is the specific reason the moment attribute survives into v1.

6. Design decisions

FICTITIOUS DATA

Six decisions. The wireframe is frozen as of this test, so all six land in prototype v1.

  1. We are reopening the Spirit question. Because T09 succeeded in only 3 of 11 runs with 3 give-ups, because all three give-ups happened on the home screen after the participant had already been inside two or three categories, because T09 collected 19 backtracks against 20 for the other nine tasks combined, and because the first click scattered across three different branches with none of them reliably wrong — and because our card-sort report named this exact condition as the one that would reopen the decision — we will build a sixth top-level branch for it into v1 and test it there. We are not claiming the card sort was wrong: one sorter in eight made a Spirit pile and that is still true. We are saying the sort measured what people group by and the tree test measured what they look for, and those turned out to be different. What separates 120 from 65

Both teams put the same situation in front of a participant. Report A’s T09 and Report B’s B10 are almost the same sentence: a woman in your class says she can never tell whether a feeling is the Spirit or just her own thoughts — find something that would help her. It is the hardest thing either structure was asked to do, and neither structure did it. That is where the two reports stop resembling each other.

Team A measured it. 3 of 11 found the block, 3 gave up, average 88.8 seconds, 19 backtracks in 7 runs, all three abandonments happening on the home screen after the participant had already been inside two or three categories. Then they diagnosed it: the first click scattered three ways with none of them reliably wrong, which is the signature of a missing branch rather than a bad label. Then they spent it. Their card-sort report had named the exact condition that would reopen the Spirit question; the tree test met the condition; the decision reopens, and prototype v1 gets a sixth branch to test. A finding travelled from one study through a prototype into another study and out the far side as a design commitment.

Team B keyed it to C15, marked all six participants wrong, and wrote that the task was probably worded badly. They were not careless about it — they noticed the pattern in the data (three people volunteered that C14 was “the closest thing”), they reported it honestly, and they took the blame themselves. The failure is upstream of effort. Nobody on the team ever asked whether the answer key was right, so six participants’ worth of evidence about a real gap in their structure was filed under “bad task” and thrown away. The same thing happened to B07, where the keyed answer is a block their wireframe does not contain: the study measured the key, and the team read the measurement as a fact about the world.

Same moment, opposite epistemics. That is the 55 points, and almost none of it is effort or polish.

If there are three habits to take from this page, they are the three the rubrics keep paying for. Build the instrument before you need the answer: Team A’s give-up button, randomized order, persisted runs and CSV export are why their table can be re-derived by a stranger, and Team B’s page-local click log is why theirs cannot. Go past the pass rate: T01 passed 10 of 11 and still had a defect, priced in seconds rather than errors; three label-echo tasks passed 18 of 18 and proved nothing at all. Cite the finding in the decision — “Because [result], we will [decision]” — including the keeps, and especially when the finding argues against something you already built. Team A reopened a call they had defended in writing two weeks earlier. Team B wrote “we feel good about our categories” over a 41.7% success rate. Nothing else on these two pages costs as much as that one sentence.

In this chain, Report A is binding. Its six decisions — reopen the Spirit branch, make category multi-valued starting with 006.title_number2, relabel the two preparation groups, rewrite the leaf labels inside “Asking so you get a real answer,” keep the five categories, keep the lesson-moment attribute — are what the wireframes’ successor implements. Both wireframes are frozen as of this study, so every citation above stays checkable; prototype v1 is where the changes land, and the five-user task test is what will judge them.
