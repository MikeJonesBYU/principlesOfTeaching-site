Open card sort: how do teachers organize the skills?

The live site organizes 53 teaching skills the way the manual does — five named principles. That is the structure we’re unimpressed by, and this is the first study of the redesign: before we draw a single box of the new structure, we watch nine people organize the raw material themselves.

Scope note: what here is real

Real: the 24 cards — verbatim skills from the Church’s Teacher Development Skills manual, exactly as they appear on the live site (see the deck appendix for the traceability key). Real too are the design decisions at the end: the wireframe (next in the chain, not yet built) really implements them, and the redesigned site that eventually ships really descends from them.

Invented: everything in between. The ward, the nine participants, every session, every quote, every number. Nobody ran this study. What’s real and what’s not →

The method

An open card sort puts your content — one item per card — in front of people from your real population and asks them to group it however makes sense to them, then name each group. It answers five questions:

  • Schemes. What organizing principle do people reach for — topic, task, audience, sequence? You may expect topic and watch everyone sort by task.
  • Vocabulary. What do they call these groups? The labels they write are candidate navigation labels.
  • Strong pairs. Which cards travel together in nearly every sort? Those belong together in the IA, whatever else you decide.
  • Splitters. Which cards scatter, landing somewhere different in every sort? Those items need a better name, multiple homes, or a facet.
  • Mental-model mismatch. Where does the users’ grouping disagree with yours? The disagreement is the finding.

It does not answer whether people can find things in the structure you eventually build — that is the tree test, which is the next study in this chain. Sort first, structure second, tree-test third.

We ran it Spencer’s way, compressed: pick cards from the content inventory in plain language with no category names as cards; recruit 8–10 people from the real population, some sorting in pairs (two people negotiating a grouping think aloud naturally, and the negotiation is where mental models surface); open sort first (“group these however makes sense to you, then name each group” — no hints, no correcting); capture everything — photograph the final sort, transcribe the group names verbatim, note the sorting principle each participant seemed to use, and note what they say while they hesitate, because a card someone holds for ten seconds is data. Then analyze for patterns, not averages.

Study design

Research question. How do the teachers this site is for naturally group and label its basic block of information — a single skill — and where do their groupings disagree with the manual’s five-principle structure, and with each other?

The deck. 24 cards drawn from the real 53-skill inventory, sampled across all five manual sections (5 / 5 / 5 / 5 / 4), each card carrying one skill’s wording verbatim. One deliberate deviation from the method as taught, worth being honest about: Spencer says 30–60 cards, and below 30 the patterns run thin. We sorted 24 because our sessions had to fit in the twenty-odd minutes a volunteer has between meetings, and because a first-pass answer across all five sections mattered more to us than a dense answer over three. The cost is real — thinner patterns, and eleven skills per section unheard from — and we accepted it with eyes open.

Participants. Nine members of the Timpanogos Shadows Ward teaching corps, producing eight sorts — seven solo and one pair. Recruited to span callings (Primary, Sunday School, Young Women, Relief Society), tenure (8 months to 41 years), and age (24 to 74).

Protocol. Card sorts on the Relief Society room table after church, one participant (or pair) at a time, 15–25 minutes each. Physical cards, shuffled between sessions. Open sort; piles named by the participant, in their words, written by them on a sticky note; we photographed each finished sort, transcribed labels verbatim, and kept running notes of hesitations and comments. No starter categories, no hints, no correcting.

Analysis

FICTITIOUS DATA The trends below are computed from the eight invented sorts above — the numbers are internally consistent, but nothing here was measured from real people.

Trends

1. Situations beat structure. Five of eight sorts organized by teaching situation or task (“Getting them to talk,” “Keeping their attention,” “Handling questions”). One sorted by lesson sequence, one by her own relationship to each skill, and exactly one — Curtis, who knows the manual best — tried to reproduce the manual’s five principles on purpose (“there’s a right answer to this, you know”). Even he managed it for only two sections: his “By the Spirit” pile stalled at two of the five Spirit-section cards in the deck, and his last seven cards became a catch-all he shrugged at: “These last ones are all just… teaching.” INVENTED QUOTE

2. The mismatch with the manual is precise, not total. Two manual sections survived as mental categories in six of eight sorts: Teaching about Jesus Christ and knowing and loving the students. The other three — Teach By the Spirit, Teach the Doctrine, Invite Diligent Learning — dissolved into situations. A pattern worth saying carefully: the manual’s who-sections (about Christ, about the students) match how teachers think; its how-sections do not. Only one sort in eight produced a Spirit pile at all.

3. Strong pairs. Three clusters never (or almost never) separated: the Christ-application cluster — C01+C05 together in 8 of 8 sorts, with C15 joining in 7; the discussion cluster — C11, C12, C14, C21 pairwise together in 6 or 7 of 8; and the personal-ministry pair C08+C10 (pray for students / message a parent), together in 6 of 8. Whatever we build, these travel together.

4. Splitters — and what kind of splitters. Seven cards landed in four or more different kinds of home across the seven scheme-forming sorts; the worst was C19 (“Answer without speculating”) with five. But the interesting case is C13 (sacred music) and C20 (open with a personal circumstance): they sat in the same pile in six of eight sorts while that pile’s meaning changed under them — “Keeping their attention” (S1), “Openers” (S3), “By the Spirit” (S4), “Opening minutes” (S5). Two organizing questions are active in people’s heads at once: what problem does this solve and when in the lesson does it happen. That is not noise to be averaged away; it is evidence that these cards need more than one home.

5. Vocabulary. The harvested labels are plain, situational, and second-person: people, talk, attention, ready, Sunday. Nobody wrote “principle,” “doctrine,” or “diligent.” The one exception proves the rule: Lonnie, the retired schoolteacher, labeled in professional vocabulary (“Questioning strategies,” “Engagement”) and then disowned it herself: “Twenty years of faculty meetings, that’s what those words are.” INVENTED QUOTE

Isolated incidents

  • Trent held the music card for a long ten seconds — “We never sing in my class. It still belongs somewhere, doesn’t it?” INVENTED QUOTE — before filing it under content. A card can be real and still homeless in one teacher’s practice.
  • Two sorters independently invented a pile for hard moments (Trent’s “Handling questions,” Whitney’s “When I don’t know what to say”) — and the deck’s biggest splitter, C19, is exactly that kind of card. Two of eight is not a category; it is a flag we keep.
  • Cami, eight months into her first calling, put seven of the 24 cards in a pile named “I don’t know what this means” — “I don’t know what a search question is. Is that a church word or a real word?” INVENTED QUOTE The newest teacher could not parse nearly a third of the manual’s own skill names.
  • In the pair sort, the negotiation was the data: Kade wanted “resist answering everything yourself” filed under grown-up theory; Rylee overruled him from practice: “If you don’t answer a seven-year-old, they’ll answer themselves, and you will not like it.” INVENTED QUOTE It stayed in “Getting answers out of them.”

And one thing that is deliberately not a category: the lesson moment. Jenae’s whole sort, Whitney’s “Openers,” and the C13/C20/C22 splitter pattern all say the same thing — when in the lesson is a second way teachers reach for a skill, cutting across every category above. We are recording it as a facet on every skill (opening / during / closing / after), not as a sixth box. One card, more than one home, selected by attribute — not filed by hand.

What we saw and are ignoring — for now, maybe forever

  • Cami’s whole scheme. “Things I already do / want to try / don’t understand” is a real mental model — but it is an organization of her relationship to the skills, not of the skills. A shared site can’t ship it as navigation. It could someday become personalization (“my skills”); parked.
  • A “hard moments” category. Two of eight sorts, plus the C19 splitter. Not enough to act on; the tree test carries the question forward.
  • After-class follow-through. One explicit pile (Trent’s), C24 wandering in four kinds of home. The lesson-moment facet’s “after” value may absorb this; watching, not building.
  • Lonnie’s labels. “Questioning strategies” is a better-sounding label than “Getting people to talk” — and it is exactly the label our population does not use. We are ignoring our most articulate labeler on purpose, and that discipline is the point of harvesting vocabulary at all.
  • The missing Spirit category. The boldest omission: one sort in eight produced one. We are not forcing a category the sorters didn’t make — but this is the site for a church whose teaching manual is organized around it, so the tree test must probe whether people look for it even though they don’t sort by it. If they do, this decision reopens.
  • Age of audience as a facet. The Primary flavor of three sorts hints at it. One facet at a time; deferred.

Design decisions feeding the wireframe

These are live commitments, not summary — the 2–3 level wireframe (next in the chain — not yet built) implements exactly this list, and nothing not on it.

  1. Because five categories emerged across six or more sorts (three of them in teacher wording no manual section uses), the wireframe’s top level is those five categories, with the harvested labels verbatim.
  2. Because C13, C20, and C22 kept their pile-mates while their pile’s meaning changed — and one sorter organized the entire deck by lesson sequence — every skill block gets two attributes: situation and moment. Pages select on attributes; no skill is filed anywhere by hand. One source, multiple views, from the first rung.
  3. Because the Christ cluster held in 8 of 8 sorts and a Christ pile appeared in 6 of 8, we KEEP a topical Teaching-about-Jesus-Christ category — an evidence-backed decision to preserve part of the manual’s own structure, not an oversight.
  4. Because the discussion cluster (C11/C12/C14/C21) and the personal-ministry pair (C08/C10) almost never separated, they are grouped as units inside their categories — the wireframe may rearrange much, but not these.
  5. Because the newest teacher couldn’t parse 7 of 24 skill names, category and group labels use only harvested vocabulary; the verbatim skill names appear one level down, where they arrive with context around them. Whether that is enough — and whether anyone goes looking for the Spirit — are the tree test’s first two tasks.
