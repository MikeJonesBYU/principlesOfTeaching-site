Open card sort: two turn-ins, graded

An open card sort puts your content — one card per item — in front of people from your real population, who group the cards however makes sense to them and then name each group in their own words. The assignment: run one with 8–10 participants on your project’s content, and turn in a single PDF with the participants’ names, pictures of every finished sort, a page of analysis (trends and isolated incidents), a page of categories and labels justified from the results, and half a page on what you’re choosing to ignore. This is Turn-in 1, graded on the 60-point Study Report Rubric — Methods 15, Results & Analysis 20, Design Decisions 25 — the same rubric every user study in the chain is graded on (the prototypes have their own). The grader’s notes below quote the rubric’s band language as it is applied.

3. Analysis: trends and isolated incidents

Trends

1. People sorted by situations, not by the manual. Five of eight sorts organized around teaching situations or tasks. One sorted by lesson sequence, one by her own relationship to each skill, and exactly one person — Curtis, who knows the manual best — tried to reproduce the manual’s five sections on purpose (“There’s a right answer to this, you know” INVENTED QUOTE). Even he only got two sections to work. His Spirit pile stalled at two cards, and his last seven cards became a catch-all: “These last ones are all just… teaching.” INVENTED QUOTE

2. The mismatch with the manual is specific, not total. Two of the manual’s five sections did show up as piles, in six of eight sorts each: teaching about Jesus Christ, and knowing/loving the students. The other three — Spirit, Doctrine, Diligent Learning — dissolved into situations. Our read: the sections about who match how teachers think, and the sections about how don’t. Only one sort in eight had a Spirit pile at all.

3. Strong pairs. We tallied card co-occurrence across all eight sorts in a spreadsheet. Three clusters basically never separate: C01+C05 were in the same pile in 8 of 8 sorts (C15 joins them in 7); the discussion cards C11, C12, C14, C21 are pairwise together in 6–7 of 8; and C08+C10 (pray for students / message a parent) are together in 6 of 8. Whatever structure we build, these stay together.

4. Splitter cards — and two different kinds. Seven cards landed in four or more different kinds of pile across the seven scheme-forming sorts; C19 (“answer without speculating”) was the worst at five. But the case we kept coming back to is C13 (sacred music) and C20 (open with a personal circumstance): they sat in the same pile in six of eight sorts while the pile’s meaning changed around them — “Keeping their attention” (S1), “Openers” (S3), “By the Spirit” (S4), “Opening minutes” (S5). We think people are answering two different questions at once — what problem does this solve and when in the lesson does it happen — and cards like these need to be findable both ways, not filed once.

5. Vocabulary. The labels people wrote are plain, situational, and second-person: talk, attention, ready, Sunday, kids. Nobody wrote “principle,” “doctrine,” or “diligent.” The one exception actually supports the point: Lonnie, the retired schoolteacher, labeled in professional vocabulary (“Questioning strategies,” “Engagement”) and then disowned it herself: “Twenty years of faculty meetings, that’s what those words are.” INVENTED QUOTE

Isolated incidents

  • Trent held the music card for a good ten seconds — “We never sing in my class. It still belongs somewhere, doesn’t it?” INVENTED QUOTE A card can be real content and still be homeless in one teacher’s actual practice.
  • Two sorters independently invented a pile for hard moments (Trent’s “Handling questions,” Whitney’s “When I don’t know what to say”) — and our biggest splitter, C19, is exactly that kind of card. Two of eight isn’t a category, but we’re writing it down.
  • Cami, eight months into her first calling, put seven of the 24 cards into “I don’t know what this means” — “Is search question a church word or a real word?” INVENTED QUOTE The newest teacher couldn’t parse almost a third of the official skill names.
  • In the pair sort, the argument was the interesting part: Kade wanted “resist answering everything yourself” filed as grown-up theory; Rylee overruled him from experience — “If you don’t answer a seven-year-old, they’ll answer themselves, and you will not like it.” INVENTED QUOTE

Grader · Results & Analysis · 20/20 — Full Marks

Go back and reread your own trend 4 — it’s the best paragraph in this report, and I want you to know why. You looked at disagreement and asked what it means instead of averaging it away. That is analysis. Throughout, you synthesized across participants (every claim carries a count) and you stayed calibrated: you found two manual sections alive and said exactly that, instead of rounding up to “the manual is wrong.” And you kept incidents separate from trends, which is precisely the distinction the assignment is teaching. Full marks.

5. What we’re ignoring — for now, maybe forever

  • Cami’s whole scheme (“already do / want to try / don’t understand”). It’s a real mental model, but it organizes her relationship to the skills, not the skills. You can’t ship that as shared navigation. Maybe personalization someday. Parked.
  • A “hard moments” category. Two of eight sorts plus the C19 splitter. Not enough to act on yet — we’re handing the question to the tree test.

1. Participants

For our card sort we used five people from our ward: Margie, Curtis, Kade, Rylee, and Cami. We were trying for more but it was a busy week for everyone. Margie and Curtis each did their sort on their own, and to save time the last three (Kade, Rylee, and Cami) did theirs together as a group. Grader · Methods · contributes to 10/15

First, thank you for reporting your protocol honestly — every problem I’m about to name is one you told me yourself, and that candor is worth more than it feels like right now. Now the problems. Five participants against an ask of 8–10 is the band’s own words: “recruited more than half of the required participants, but not all.” Showing example groups before an open sort is priming — your significant error — because the whole point of an open sort is to learn what categories people invent, and you handed them two. Three people sorting together produces one negotiated sort, not three (so you really have three data points, not five). And removing three cards after your first participant means your sessions aren’t comparable — the instrument changed mid-study. Developing, 10/15, and near its floor.

Grader · Methods + R&A · the significant error

“Capture everything” isn’t a nicety in this method — it’s what makes your analysis checkable. The group session was your biggest single source of data, and it has no record at all except memory — and memory always agrees with you. That fails the rubric’s appendix requirement: “raw data incomplete or hard to connect to the analysis.” The quieter loss is the one that hurts more: your participants wrote pile names in their own words, and you paraphrased them away — “groups about discussion,” “mostly about kids.” Those verbatim labels are the most valuable thing a card sort produces; they are candidate navigation text. Next time: photograph every sort before anyone touches the table, and transcribe every label exactly as written.

3. Analysis

Margie sorted really fast and made five groups, mostly about class problems. Curtis took his time and tried to use the manual’s organization and mostly could. The group (Kade, Rylee, and Cami) made groups that were mostly about kids — Kade did most of the talking and the other two mostly agreed with him. Cami said she was confused by a lot of the cards, like she didn’t know what a search question was.

Overall the main takeaway is that nobody really understands the manual’s organization and everyone thinks in terms of problems instead — all three of the group agreed about that. Also people don’t read very carefully, which shows how the younger generation just skims things. So the sorts basically prove the site should be reorganized around problems, since that’s what people want.

Grader · Results & Analysis · 13/20 — Developing (capped)

Your first paragraph is a recap, not analysis: one sentence per session, zero patterns. The rubric’s words: “results accurate but presented participant-by-participant with little synthesis.” Then the second paragraph over-claims in three directions, and here’s what I need you to see: your own recap contradicts you. “Nobody understands the manual’s organization” — two sentences earlier you told me Curtis “mostly could.” “All three of the group agreed” is not three findings — a negotiated sort is one data point, and you told me yourself that Kade did most of the talking. And a claim about “the younger generation” hung on one participant’s vocabulary question isn’t a finding; it’s a stereotype with n=1. Over-claiming caps a submission at this level regardless of polish — 13/20. One more thing, gently: your conclusions are about categories and problems, and you showed your participants example categories before they sorted. You can’t un-ring that bell in the analysis.

4. Categories and labels

Based on our study we decided to go with seven categories: Teaching Fundamentals, Spiritual Teaching, Engagement, Questions, Preparation, Love, and Misc. We feel like these cover everything and match how the manual is set up while still being about problems. We might tweak the names later to be more catchy.

Grader · Design Decisions · 8/25 — Beginning

Stop and look at what happened here, because it’s the most common failure in this assignment and it’s fixable: not one of these seven categories cites a result, and several argue with your own data. “Questions” and “Preparation” are your starter examples coming back to you — you handed those to your participants in element 1, so they’re an echo, not a finding. Nothing in your three sorts produced anything like “Teaching Fundamentals.” The only support for “Spiritual Teaching” anywhere in your report is Curtis trying to reproduce the manual. “Misc” commits to nothing. And “match how the manual is set up” — one sentence after your analysis said nobody understands the manual’s organization — tells me this plan existed before the study ran. That’s the Beginning band: “decisions ignore or contradict the data.” The fix is not more categories; it’s a sentence shape. “Because [result], we will [decision]” — for every single row. If you can’t fill in the “because,” the decision isn’t ready.

Honestly nothing major — the study mostly confirmed what we already expected going in.

Grader · Element 5 · the tell

“Nothing major” is the tell, and I want to be direct about it: a study that only confirms what you expected is a study you haven’t looked at yet. The surprising material was right there in your own sessions. Which three cards did Margie find so confusing that you pulled them — and why? That was a finding, and you threw it off the table. This element exists to make you go find those things, and it would have saved you points above: if you’d seriously asked “what are we choosing to ignore?”, the contradictions in element 4 would have surfaced on their own. What separates 60 from 31

Report B’s trouble started before the first card hit the table — five recruits, a primed deck, a group sort counted as three voices — but notice where the points actually went: 5 lost on methods, 24 on the reading and the deciding. If I could get every team to internalize three habits, they would be these. Verbatim capture: photos, labels, quotes — without them your analysis is memory, and memory always agrees with you. Synthesis with calibration: patterns with counts, claims no bigger than the data, and disagreement treated as signal rather than noise. Cited decisions: “because six of eight… we will…” — including the courage to keep what the data defends and to omit what it doesn’t, even when the manual disagrees with you. The rubric’s weighting says the same thing: 15 points for running the study, 45 for what you make of it.

In this chain, Report A is binding. Its element 4 — five categories, harvested labels, the situation + moment attributes — is exactly what the 2–3 level wireframe implements next, and the tree test will inherit its open questions (does anyone look for the Spirit? do the harvested labels carry?).
