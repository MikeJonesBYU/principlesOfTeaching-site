Preface: the method

An open card sort puts your content — one item per card — in front of people from your real population and asks them to group it however makes sense to them, then name each group. It answers five questions:

  • Schemes. What organizing principle do people reach for — topic, task, audience, sequence? You may expect topic and watch everyone sort by task.
  • Vocabulary. What do they call these groups? The labels they write are candidate navigation labels.
  • Strong pairs. Which cards travel together in nearly every sort? Those belong together in the IA, whatever else you decide.
  • Splitters. Which cards scatter, landing somewhere different in every sort? Those items need a better name, multiple homes, or a facet.
  • Mental-model mismatch. Where does the users’ grouping disagree with yours? The disagreement is the finding.

It does not answer whether people can find things in the structure you eventually build — that is the tree test, next in this chain. Sort first, structure second, tree-test third.

Run it Spencer’s way, compressed: pick cards from the content inventory in plain language, no category names as cards; recruit 8–10 people from the real population, some sorting in pairs (two people negotiating a grouping think aloud naturally, and the negotiation is where mental models surface); open sort first — “group these however makes sense to you, then name each group,” no hints, no correcting; capture everything — photograph every finished sort, transcribe group names verbatim, note each participant’s apparent sorting principle and what they say while hesitating, because a card someone holds for ten seconds is data. Then analyze for patterns, not averages.

The assignment

As given to the class — what to turn in: a single PDF containing five elements.

  1. The names of the people who were in your card sorting study.
  2. Pictures of the resulting card sorts for each participant.
  3. One page or less of analysis in which you discuss trends you saw in the sorts and specific isolated incidents that you saw.
  4. One page or less describing the categories and labels you ended up with and a justification based on the results and analysis. Don’t overthink this.
  5. Half a page or less about what you saw in the results or analysis that you are going to ignore for now and maybe forever. This is to get you thinking critically about the results of your study.

3. Analysis: trends and isolated incidents

Trends

1. Situations beat structure. Five of eight sorts organized by teaching situation or task. One sorted by lesson sequence, one by her own relationship to each skill, and exactly one — Curtis, who knows the manual best — tried to reproduce the manual’s five principles on purpose (“There’s a right answer to this, you know” INVENTED QUOTE). Even he managed only two sections; his Spirit pile stalled at two cards and his last seven became a catch-all: “These last ones are all just… teaching.” INVENTED QUOTE

2. The mismatch with the manual is precise, not total. Two manual sections survived as mental categories in six of eight sorts: Teaching about Jesus Christ and knowing and loving the students. The other three — Spirit, Doctrine, Diligent Learning — dissolved into situations. The who-sections match how teachers think; the how-sections do not. Only one sort in eight produced a Spirit pile at all.

3. Strong pairs. The Christ-application cluster: C01+C05 together in 8 of 8 sorts, C15 joining in 7. The discussion cluster: C11, C12, C14, C21 pairwise together in 6–7 of 8. The personal-ministry pair C08+C10: 6 of 8. Whatever we build, these travel together.

4. Splitters — and what kind. Seven cards landed in four or more kinds of home; C19 (“answer without speculating”) was worst at five. The interesting case: C13 (sacred music) and C20 (open with a personal circumstance) sat in the same pile in six of eight sorts while that pile’s meaning changed under them — attention (S1), opener (S3, S5), Spirit (S4). Two organizing questions are active at once: what problem does this solve, and when in the lesson does it happen. That is not noise; it is evidence these cards need more than one home.

5. Vocabulary. Harvested labels are plain, situational, second-person: people, talk, attention, ready, Sunday. Nobody wrote “principle,” “doctrine,” or “diligent.” The exception proves the rule: Lonnie, the retired schoolteacher, labeled in professional vocabulary and disowned it herself — “Twenty years of faculty meetings, that’s what those words are.” INVENTED QUOTE

Isolated incidents

  • Trent held the music card a long ten seconds — “We never sing in my class. It still belongs somewhere, doesn’t it?” INVENTED QUOTE A card can be real and still homeless in one teacher’s practice.
  • Two sorters independently invented a hard-moments pile (“Handling questions,” “When I don’t know what to say”) — and the biggest splitter, C19, is exactly that kind of card. Two of eight is not a category; it is a flag we keep.
  • Cami, eight months in, put seven of 24 cards in “I don’t know what this means” — “Is search question a church word or a real word?” INVENTED QUOTE The newest teacher couldn’t parse a third of the manual’s own skill names.
  • In the pair sort the negotiation was the data: Kade wanted “resist answering everything yourself” filed as grown-up theory; Rylee overruled him from practice — “If you don’t answer a seven-year-old, they’ll answer themselves, and you will not like it.” INVENTED QUOTE

Grader · Results & Analysis · 20/20 — Full Marks

Synthesis across participants, not a session-by-session recap: every trend is a pattern with a count, and disagreement is analyzed as signal rather than averaged away (trend 4 is the best paragraph in the report). Claims stay calibrated — note what trend 2 does not claim: it found two manual sections alive and says so, instead of rounding up to “the manual is wrong.” Incidents are kept separate from trends, which is exactly the assignment’s distinction.

5. What we’re ignoring — for now, maybe forever

  • Cami’s whole scheme (“already do / want to try / don’t understand”) — a real mental model, but of her relationship to the skills, not of the skills. Maybe personalization someday; parked.
  • A “hard moments” category — two of eight sorts plus the C19 splitter. Not enough to act on; the tree test carries the question.
  • After-class follow-through — one explicit pile; C24 wandering. The moment facet’s “after” value may absorb it; watching, not building.
  • Lonnie’s labels — “Questioning strategies” sounds better than “Getting people to talk” and is exactly what our population does not say. We ignore our most articulate labeler on purpose.

  • Age of audience as a facet — hinted by the Primary flavor of three sorts. One facet at a time; deferred.

3. Analysis

Margie sorted fast and made five groups, mostly about class problems. Trent made seven groups, which was the most, and his were more organized. Whitney made six groups including one about not knowing what to say. Curtis tried to use the manual’s organization and mostly could. Jenae sorted by the timeline of her week. Kade and Rylee did theirs together and made kid-themed groups. Lonnie’s were the most professional-sounding. Cami took the longest and was confused by a lot of the cards.

Overall: nobody understands the manual’s organization, and everyone thinks in problems instead. Also, people don’t read carefully — Cami said she didn’t know what a search question is, which shows the younger generation skims. The sorts prove the site needs to be reorganized around problems, which is what people want.

Grader · Results & Analysis · 13/20 — Developing (capped)

The first paragraph is a session-by-session recap — eight accurate sentences and not one pattern: “results accurate but presented participant-by-participant with little synthesis.” The second paragraph then over-claims in three directions at once: “nobody understands the manual’s organization” (the report’s own recap says Curtis “mostly could,” and two manual sections appeared in six of eight sorts); “everyone thinks in problems” (Jenae sorted by sequence, Cami by self-relevance — both in the recap above); and a generational claim hung on one participant’s vocabulary question. Over-claiming caps a submission at this level regardless of polish. The real findings this study produced — the strong pairs, the splitter cards, the label harvest — go unmentioned; the data was collected and then not read.

Based on our study we are going with seven categories: Teaching Fundamentals, Spiritual Teaching, Engagement, Classroom Discussion, Preparation, Love, and Misc. Not one category cites a result. The set “matches how the manual is set up” — after a study whose one loud finding was that it shouldn’t — which is the signature of a plan made before the study ran. “May adjust the names later” defers the exact question the card sort exists to answer.

What separates 60 from 31

Not the study. The same nine people made the same piles on the same table. Report A captured everything and read it; Report B captured some of it and recognized it. Three habits carry the 29 points: verbatim capture (photos, labels, quotes — without them the analysis is memory), synthesis with calibration (patterns with counts, claims no bigger than the data, disagreement treated as signal), and cited decisions (“because six of eight… we will…” — including the courage to keep what the data defends and to omit what it doesn’t, even when the manual disagrees). The rubric’s weighting says the same thing: 15 points for running it, 45 for what you make of it.

In this chain, Report A is binding. Its element 4 — five categories, harvested labels, the situation + moment facets — is exactly what the 2–3 level wireframe implements next, and the tree test will inherit its open questions (does anyone look for the Spirit? do the harvested labels carry?).
