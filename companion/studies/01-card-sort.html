Preface: the method

An open card sort puts your content — one item per card — in front of people from your real population and asks them to group it however makes sense to them, then name each group. It answers five questions:

  • Schemes. What organizing principle do people reach for — topic, task, audience, sequence? You may expect topic and watch everyone sort by task.
  • Vocabulary. What do they call these groups? The labels they write are candidate navigation labels.
  • Strong pairs. Which cards travel together in nearly every sort? Those belong together in the IA, whatever else you decide.
  • Splitters. Which cards scatter, landing somewhere different in every sort? Those items need a better name, multiple homes, or a facet.
  • Mental-model mismatch. Where does the users’ grouping disagree with yours? The disagreement is the finding.

It does not answer whether people can find things in the structure you eventually build — that is the tree test, next in this chain. Sort first, structure second, tree-test third.

Run it Spencer’s way, compressed: pick cards from the content inventory in plain language, no category names as cards; recruit 8–10 people from the real population, some sorting in pairs (two people negotiating a grouping think aloud naturally, and the negotiation is where mental models surface); open sort first — “group these however makes sense to you, then name each group,” no hints, no correcting; capture everything — photograph every finished sort, transcribe group names verbatim, note each participant’s apparent sorting principle and what they say while hesitating, because a card someone holds for ten seconds is data. Then analyze for patterns, not averages.

The assignment

As given to the class — what to turn in: a single PDF containing five elements.

  1. The names of the people who were in your card sorting study.
  2. Pictures of the resulting card sorts for each participant.
  3. One page or less of analysis in which you discuss trends you saw in the sorts and specific isolated incidents that you saw.
  4. One page or less describing the categories and labels you ended up with and a justification based on the results and analysis. Don’t overthink this.
  5. Half a page or less about what you saw in the results or analysis that you are going to ignore for now and maybe forever. This is to get you thinking critically about the results of your study.

3. Analysis: trends and isolated incidents

Trends

1. People sorted by situations, not by the manual. Five of eight sorts organized around teaching situations or tasks. One sorted by lesson sequence, one by her own relationship to each skill, and exactly one person — Curtis, who knows the manual best — tried to reproduce the manual’s five sections on purpose (“There’s a right answer to this, you know” INVENTED QUOTE). Even he only got two sections to work. His Spirit pile stalled at two cards, and his last seven cards became a catch-all: “These last ones are all just… teaching.” INVENTED QUOTE

2. The mismatch with the manual is specific, not total. Two of the manual’s five sections did show up as piles, in six of eight sorts each: teaching about Jesus Christ, and knowing/loving the students. The other three — Spirit, Doctrine, Diligent Learning — dissolved into situations. Our read: the sections about who match how teachers think, and the sections about how don’t. Only one sort in eight had a Spirit pile at all.

3. Strong pairs. We tallied card co-occurrence across all eight sorts in a spreadsheet. Three clusters basically never separate: C01+C05 were in the same pile in 8 of 8 sorts (C15 joins them in 7); the discussion cards C11, C12, C14, C21 are pairwise together in 6–7 of 8; and C08+C10 (pray for students / message a parent) are together in 6 of 8. Whatever structure we build, these stay together.

4. Splitter cards — and two different kinds. Seven cards landed in four or more different kinds of pile across the seven scheme-forming sorts; C19 (“answer without speculating”) was the worst at five. But the case we kept coming back to is C13 (sacred music) and C20 (open with a personal circumstance): they sat in the same pile in six of eight sorts while the pile’s meaning changed around them — “Keeping their attention” (S1), “Openers” (S3), “By the Spirit” (S4), “Opening minutes” (S5). We think people are answering two different questions at once — what problem does this solve and when in the lesson does it happen — and cards like these need to be findable both ways, not filed once.

5. Vocabulary. The labels people wrote are plain, situational, and second-person: talk, attention, ready, Sunday, kids. Nobody wrote “principle,” “doctrine,” or “diligent.” The one exception actually supports the point: Lonnie, the retired schoolteacher, labeled in professional vocabulary (“Questioning strategies,” “Engagement”) and then disowned it herself: “Twenty years of faculty meetings, that’s what those words are.” INVENTED QUOTE

  • Trent held the music card for a good ten seconds — “We never sing in my class. It still belongs somewhere, doesn’t it?” INVENTED QUOTE A card can be real content and still be homeless in one teacher’s actual practice.
  • Two sorters independently invented a pile for hard moments (Trent’s “Handling questions,” Whitney’s “When I don’t know what to say”) — and our biggest splitter, C19, is exactly that kind of card. Two of eight isn’t a category, but we’re writing it down.
  • Cami, eight months into her first calling, put seven of the 24 cards into “I don’t know what this means” — “Is search question a church word or a real word?” INVENTED QUOTE The newest teacher couldn’t parse almost a third of the official skill names.
  • In the pair sort, the argument was the interesting part: Kade wanted “resist answering everything yourself” filed as grown-up theory; Rylee overruled him from experience — “If you don’t answer a seven-year-old, they’ll answer themselves, and you will not like it.” INVENTED QUOTE

5. What we’re ignoring — for now, maybe forever

  • Cami’s whole scheme (“already do / want to try / don’t understand”). It’s a real mental model, but it organizes her relationship to the skills, not the skills. You can’t ship that as shared navigation. Maybe personalization someday. Parked.
  • A “hard moments” category. Two of eight sorts plus the C19 splitter. Not enough to act on yet — we’re handing the question to the tree test.
  • After-class follow-through. One explicit pile, and C24 wandered everywhere. The moment attribute’s “after” value may cover it. Watching, not building.
  • Lonnie’s labels. Honestly, “Questioning strategies” sounds better than “Getting people to talk” — and it’s exactly the language our population does not use. We’re ignoring our most articulate labeler on purpose, which felt wrong and we’re pretty sure is right.

  • Age of audience as a facet. The Primary flavor of three sorts hints at it. One facet at a time. Deferred.

What separates 60 from 31

Not the study. The same nine people made the same piles on the same table. If I could get every team to internalize three habits, they would be these. Verbatim capture: photos, labels, quotes — without them your analysis is memory, and memory always agrees with you. Synthesis with calibration: patterns with counts, claims no bigger than the data, and disagreement treated as signal rather than noise. Cited decisions: “because six of eight… we will…” — including the courage to keep what the data defends and to omit what it doesn’t, even when the manual disagrees with you.
