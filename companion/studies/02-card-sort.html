CS 356 Companion · Study 2 · Open card sort

Nobody rebuilt the manual’s five sections

We put twenty-one of the manual’s skills on cards — the skill’s own words, nothing rewritten — and asked five teachers to drag them into groups and name the groups themselves. Not one rebuilt the five sections the manual is organized by. They built situations: getting them to talk, running out of time, hard questions, knowing the class.

This page is deliberately short. It is the shape your own P1 card-sort report should have: every sort shown, a page of analysis, half a page of decisions, and an honest list of what we decided not to act on.

The method, briefly

An open card sort hands someone a deck of content items and asks them to group it however they make sense — with no categories supplied — then to name each group.

The groupings tell you what belongs together. The titles give you the reader’s own words for those groups — the raw material for navigation labels. Five to eight sorts show the dominant scheme and the minority ones — not enough for a dendrogram. We read the sorts.

Study design

Research question

How do teachers group the manual’s skills when nobody gives them categories — and what words do they put at the top of a group?

The deck: 21 cards, each one a skill’s own words

A card holds one skill from the Teacher Development Skills guide. We sorted twenty-one of the fifty-three to keep the study tractable, spread proportionally across the five sections (5 / 4 / 4 / 4 / 4 against 12 / 11 / 10 / 10 / 10). Nothing reworded; every card carries its skill id.

The tradeoff: the manual’s wording carries its structure — five cards say “Jesus Christ” and can be collected on the words alone. Rewriting closes that leak but smuggles our decisions into the instrument, so we chose traceability (see what we ignored).

Protocol

One participant at a time on Zoom, screen shared, with a free online card-sorting site in their own browser (generic on purpose: the method does not depend on the tool).

  1. Set up (5 min). Open the link; check they can drag a card.
  2. Frame the task (2 min). “Group these however makes sense to you. You are not being tested — the manual is.” No categories, no section names.
  3. Group (15–25 min). They dragged and thought aloud; we prompted only with “what are you looking at?”
  4. Then name the groups (5–10 min). Only once the piles stopped moving did we ask for a typed title on each column. Titles after grouping, never before — a title typed first becomes a rule the rest of the sort obeys.
  5. Walk it back (5 min). They read their own sort aloud.

Sessions predate the September 2026 schedule change; nothing here turns on how long a class is.

Analysis

FICTITIOUS DATA The counts here are counts of invented sorts. The reading method is real.

What kept happening

Nobody rebuilt the manual’s five sections. Twenty-eight group titles across five sorts, not one a section name. 018.title_number1 and 018.title_number2 share a subsection, and three of five participants split them; the 029 pair stayed together on four sorts, never in a column named after that subsection.

Groups formed around situations, not subjects. Seventeen of the twenty-eight titles name a moment teaching is going a particular way — “Getting them to talk”, “Running out of time (what to cut)”, “When it gets hard”. Eight name a subject.

One situation is on every sort, under five names. Do I know these people: “Knowing the class”, “Do they feel like they belong?”, “The kids themselves”, “Relationship stuff”, “Making them feel wanted”. The same four cards move as a block on four of the five.

They wrote in their own vocabulary. Nobody typed “skill”, “principle”, or “doctrine”; nine titles open with a gerund and eight put the class in as them — the hallway vocabulary the ward page records.

What surprised us

One section did survive, and we do not trust it. Every sort has a Christ group — and Nathan titled his “Christ (all of these say Christ)”. Five cards contain the words; he sorted on the string, and swept in a sixth that never mentions Christ.

An axis the manual does not have. Three sorts organized partly by when a skill gets used: “Before Sunday — my own preparation”; “Openers”, “Middle”, “Checks”. Nobody prompted a timeline.

Typing a title moved cards. Four of five dragged a card after they started naming. Name first and the sort becomes filing against the participant’s opening guess.

Once or twice, but worth keeping

A group that means “not for me” (P13, once). Lonnie left four cards in “The ones that are for grown-up classes” — a filter on the inventory, from the sample’s only Primary teacher. Logged as a question for the tree test.

Wording that only works if you already know (P05, once). Curtis stalled on 006.title_number2: “sensory experience with scriptural objects” is nothing anyone would say in a hallway. An outside reviewer says it louder in study 5.

“Can this one go in two places?” (P06, once). The tool says no; the content says yes. Asked once, and it decided something anyway (third decision below).

Three findings we are ignoring, and why

A study produces more results than it produces evidence. What you decline to act on has to be written down where a reader can argue with it — otherwise the things you quietly dropped look like things you never saw.

  • The Christ group, as evidence about grouping. Artifact of the deck. Five cards carry the words “Jesus Christ”; a participant can collect them without reading past the second word, and one of them said so in his own title. The shipped site does have a Christ-centered category — it earns its place from the manual’s own emphasis and from study 1, not from this pile. Any deck of verbatim names will hand you a group like this. Do not spend it twice.
  • Differences in how many groups people made. Artifact of the tool and the device. Four groups on an iPad and seven on a laptop is a fact about dragging, not about how finely a teacher thinks. Curtis stopped splitting piles when his hand got tired; Jenae could make a column with a keystroke. We read which cards went together and what the columns were called, and never compared group counts across participants.
  • What was in the “Not sure” column. Artifact of the session’s end. Trent’s four leftovers were the last four cards he touched, past the thirty-minute mark. That is a signal about fatigue and about the order the tool dealt the deck, not about those four skills.

Design decisions: how skills get grouped, and what the groups are called

Everything here is real and shipped, cited to the live site and the commit.

  1. Group by situation, not by the manual’s section. Twenty-eight titles, zero section names, seventeen situations — so the site got a second front door: seven problem categories in problems.html, defined in assets/problems-data.js (e2bf223).
  2. Label in the teacher’s words. The titles were first-person and situational, so the shipped entries are complaints, not topics — “No one answers when I ask a question” — and those strings live in problems-data.js.
  3. Let a skill live in more than one place. Problems map to skills many-to-many: 018.title_number1 is filed under both “I run out of time…” and “I can’t tell whether they’re actually learning.” The sort forced a choice; the site does not.
  4. Keep the manual’s hierarchy as well. Two sorts held subject-shaped groups, and a teacher assigned a topic still needs to browse, so structure.html and select.html stay — study 3 tests whether that was right.

What changed: the site stopped having one front door — seven categories in teacher language, entries phrased as a teacher would say them, and a skill free to appear under every problem it helps with.

What we’d do differently

  • Stop letting the device pick the sample. A remote drag-and-drop sort quietly requires a laptop or tablet, and most of this ward’s teachers — nearly all of Primary — work on a phone. One Primary teacher in five sorts is a sample shaped by our tooling, not by our recruiting. Paper cards in person, or a phone-friendly tool, would have produced a different room.
  • Run a second deck. Twenty-one of fifty-three skills were sorted; thirty-two were never seen, and the seven shipped categories have to hold all fifty-three. That extrapolation is real and we made it. A closed sort of the remaining thirty-two into these categories would cost an hour and close the gap.
  • Shuffle the deck between participants. We dealt the cards in the same order every time, which is why the leftover pile is uninterpretable and why the Christ cards kept arriving in a clump. Shuffling costs nothing and would have saved a whole ignored finding.
  • Ask the Primary teacher the obvious follow-up. Lonnie set four cards aside as belonging to somebody else’s class and we let it pass. “What would have to be true for these to be for you?” was one question long, and we did not ask it.
