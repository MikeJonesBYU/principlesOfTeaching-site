CS 356 Companion · Study 2 · Open card sort

Nobody built the manual’s five sections

We printed thirty cards, one skill to a card, in the plainest language we could write. We handed the deck to ten teachers across eight sessions and asked them to make piles that made sense to them, and then to name the piles. We did not tell them what the manual’s five sections were.

Not one of the eight sorts came back with the manual’s structure. Six of the eight built piles out of situations — the moments in a lesson where something goes wrong. One built a running order. One came close to topics and then named his topics after situations anyway.

That result is the reason the live site has a problem-driven front door at all, and the reason its seven categories are named the way they are.

The method: an open card sort

Everything in this section is method, not findings. It is real, and you can copy it.

A card sort hands people the content of a site, one item per card, and asks them to organize it. In an open sort they make whatever groups they like and then name the groups themselves. In a closed sort you hand them your proposed categories and ask them to file the cards into those. The two are not variants of one method; they answer different questions. Open sorting is generative — it produces candidate structures and candidate labels. Closed sorting is evaluative — it tests a structure you already have. We had no structure worth testing yet, only the manual’s, so we ran an open sort.

The names are the point. In an open sort you are not only watching where the cards land; you are collecting a direct, unprompted sample of the words your users reach for when they have to describe a group of things out loud. That sample is the raw material of the labelling system. You cannot get it from a closed sort, because in a closed sort the labels are yours already.

Deck size runs roughly thirty to sixty cards. Below thirty there is not enough content for structure to emerge; above sixty people start managing the pile instead of thinking about it. Cards are written in plain language, one item per card, never in the designer’s vocabulary, and never as category names — a card that is secretly a label tells participants the answer.

Eight to ten participants drawn from the real population is a normal size. Donna Spencer’s advice, which we followed, is to run some of the sorts in pairs: two people sorting one deck have to negotiate out loud, and the negotiation exposes the mental model in a way a solo sorter’s silence never will. Throughout, the think-aloud is often the more valuable half of what you collect. A card someone holds for ten seconds is data. A card someone moves three times is data. Write it down.

Analysis looks for patterns, not averages. There is no mean structure to compute; you are not averaging your way to an information architecture. You standardize similar group names so that “when nobody talks” and “when they won’t answer” count as the same idea, and then read for: groups almost everybody made; strong pairs, meaning cards that travel together across sorts; splitters, meaning cards that scatter; the organizing schemes people used (topic, task, audience, sequence); and the vocabulary.

We did not build a dendrogram, deliberately. At eight sorts and thirty cards the clustering statistics are noise dressed as rigour: a cluster tree drawn from eight observations will show structure whether or not any exists, and it will hide the one sort that disagreed with everybody — which, as it turned out, was the most interesting sort we ran. Spencer’s guidance for a study this size is to standardize the group names and then eyeball the result. That is what we did. A dendrogram earns its place at thirty or fifty sorts, in an online tool, where no human can hold the data in view at once.

What a card sort cannot tell you

This matters as much as what it can. A card sort tells you how people group and name content. It does not tell you:

  • Whether users can find things.

    Grouping is not finding. People routinely build a structure they then fail to navigate. That question belongs to a tree test, which is the next study in this arc.

  • Whether a label invites the right click.

    A group name that a participant produced while holding a pile of cards may read completely differently as a link on a page with six siblings. That question belongs to a first-click test, which is why the first artifact this study produced is a navigation prototype rather than a specification.

  • Whether the content is any good.

    Every card in this deck was accepted as sensible. That says nothing about whether the skills work on a Sunday.

Sources for the method. The CS 356 course material on card sorting, and Donna Spencer, Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories (Rosenfeld Media, 2009). Both real; neither is quoted at length here.

Study design

Written as a design you could copy and run. The participants and sessions in it are invented.

Research question

When teachers organize the manual’s skills themselves, what structure do they build — and what do they call the groups? Behind it, the question the brief actually cared about: is the manual’s five-section hierarchy the structure teachers already carry, or is it the publisher’s structure?

Materials and protocol

  • Physical index cards.

    Thirty cards, handwriting-sized type, one skill per card. Numbered on the back only, so the numbers are an analysis key and never a running order the participant can read. Shuffled before every session. Blank cards and a pen were on the table for group names, and more blank cards in case anyone wanted to write a card we had missed. Nobody did.

  • Kitchen tables and a church foyer.

    Six sessions at participants’ own tables, two in the foyer after church because that is when those two people were actually available. The foyer sessions were noisier and are not obviously worse; the foyer is where this ward talks about teaching anyway.

  • The prompt, said the same way each time.

    “These are things a teacher could do. Put them into groups that make sense to you — any number of groups, and it’s fine to have a pile that doesn’t fit anywhere. When you’re done, write a name on a blank card for each group. There is no right answer, and I’m not testing you.”

  • Think-aloud, captured by hand.

    No recording. One observer, writing. That is a real limitation and we say so in what we’d do differently: hand notes catch the loud moments and lose the quiet ones, and every quotation on this page is therefore a reconstruction even within the fiction.

  • Named after sorting, never before.

    Participants were not allowed to write a group name and then fill it. Naming a pile before you build it turns an open sort into a closed one that the participant designed, and the vocabulary you collect is no longer a response to the content.

How the cards were made

The deck is the instrument. A card sort is only as good as its cards, and most of the work in this study happened before anybody sorted anything.

(a) The content inventory is real. The site’s generator pulled every skill out of the Teacher Development Skills manual into one structured file, assets/lang/eng/skills-data.js: 53 skills, each with an id, a name, the subsection it sits under, the first sentence of its “Define” section, and a deep link back to the manual page it came from. Those 53 skills are distributed across the manual’s five sections as 12 / 11 / 10 / 10 / 10. That file is the content inventory. We did not have to invent one, and neither should you if the content already exists somewhere structured.

(b) Selecting 30 of the 53. Thirty is at the bottom of the useful range, and we chose the bottom on purpose: these participants were going to do this standing at a kitchen counter after a shift, or in a foyer with their coat still on. Three rules produced the cut.

  • Proportional coverage of all five sections.

    A deck that over-samples one section pre-loads the answer. At 30 of 53 the proportional targets were 6.8 / 6.2 / 5.7 / 5.7 / 5.7, which rounds to 7 / 6 / 6 / 6 / 6 — thirty-one cards. One had to go, and it came out of Invite Diligent Learning, which contained the closest near-duplicate pair in the whole inventory (two skills that both amount to “get them studying during the week”). Final deck: 7 / 6 / 6 / 6 / 5.

  • Drop near-duplicates.

    The manual frequently states a skill twice at different grain — “create search questions to help students identify roles, titles, symbols…” and “ask questions that allow students to identify roles Jesus Christ can have in their lives” are one card’s worth of idea, not two. Two near-identical cards in a deck do not test anything; they just tell you that people put identical things together, which you already knew, and they cost you a slot.

  • Drop skills that cannot stand alone.

    Some skills are unintelligible without the manual open beside them — one names a specific evaluation document by title; another asks the teacher to “think celestial” about the class, which is a phrase rather than an action. A card that sends the participant looking for context has stopped measuring their model and started measuring their reading comprehension. Both were cut.

(c) Rewriting each card. The manual’s sentences are long, imperative, and written to be read slowly with scriptures open. A card has to be readable in about three seconds by a Primary teacher who is holding it at arm’s length. Four rules:

  • One action per card.

    If the manual’s sentence contains two verbs the participant could do separately, the card takes one of them. A card with two actions in it can legitimately go in two piles, and then you have manufactured a splitter that tells you nothing about the content.

  • Strip every word that telegraphs a section name.

    This is the rule that matters most, and it is the easiest one to break by accident. The deck must not leak the taxonomy it is testing. The word “Spirit” appears on zero of the thirty cards; so does “doctrine”; so does “love” as a label-word. Where the manual said “follow the promptings of the Holy Ghost” the card says “then do what comes.” Where it said “examples of Heavenly Father’s love,” the card says “where the passage shows God caring about someone.”

    We could not do this for the fifth section. Four of the thirty cards name the Savior, because a card drawn from Focus on Jesus Christ that does not mention Christ is no longer that skill. We knew this was a leak when we printed it, and it is the first thing we hold against our own results in the discussion.

  • Compress to card length, in ward language.

    “Learner,” “students,” and “class members” all become “them.” Nominalizations become verbs. Twenty-four words become nine. The test we applied to every card: could a teacher read this out loud in one breath and know immediately whether she has ever done it?

  • Never write a card that is a category.

    No card says “classroom management” or “lesson preparation.” Every card is a single concrete thing a teacher does on a specific Sunday. A card that is really a label hands the participant a pile name and then you collect it back and call it a finding.

  • Keep a traceability key.

    Every card keeps its source skill id, so that when a pile forms you can walk it back to real content and, later, map the shipped categories onto real skills. Without the key, a card sort produces a nice diagram and nothing you can build. The key is the table below.

Results

Eight sorts, thirty cards, 240 card placements, 44 participant-written group names. Everything in this section is invented.

FICTITIOUS DATA Nobody sorted these cards. Every pile, name, count, and quotation below was written for CS 356, constructed to be consistent with the real design decision at the end of the page. Read them as a model of how a card sort is reported, not as evidence about teachers.

Finding 1 — the scheme, and the mismatch

Nobody reproduced the manual’s five sections. Not one sort produced five groups; not one produced a group whose membership matched a manual section; not one used a section name. The manual’s structure — Focus on Jesus Christ, Love Those You Teach, Teach By the Spirit, Teach the Doctrine, Invite Diligent Learning — is a structure of topics. Six of the eight sorts built structures of situations: the moment in a lesson when something is going wrong and you need to do something about it.

The near-miss: P05 Curtis

Curtis has read the manual more carefully than anyone else in the roster, and it shows: his five piles are the closest thing in the study to the manual’s sections, with four of them holding mostly cards from a single section. Then he named them. “When it’s about Him.” “When they’re not with you.” “When they ask something hard.” He had grouped like an editor and labelled like a teacher, and when the two disagreed the teacher won. That is worth more than a clean result: the one participant with the taxonomy in his head still could not name a pile after a topic.

The genuine alternate scheme: P11 Nathan

Nathan sorted by time within the lesson, and it was not a confusion — it was a whole competing architecture, executed cleanly and laid out on the table in order: Before · First five minutes · The middle · Last two minutes · After class, during the week, plus a sixth pile off to one side that he called Doesn’t fit in twenty-five minutes.

His sort is the reason we did not draw a cluster tree. Averaged in with the other seven, Nathan disappears: his cards distribute across everyone else’s groups and add a faint, uninterpretable smear. Read on its own, he is the single most useful hour in the study — a teacher who has stopped thinking of a lesson as a topic and started thinking of it as a runtime. We set his sort aside and analyzed it separately rather than forcing it into the standardization, which is what you are supposed to do with a minority scheme that is internally coherent.

The card sort did not discover the ward’s vocabulary; it confirmed that the vocabulary survives contact with an abstraction task. Handed thirty decontextualized cards and asked to be organizational, these teachers went right on talking about last Sunday.

Finding 4 — standardizing to seven groups

Standardization is where a card sort becomes design work and stops being transcription. Forty-four names is not a structure; it is a word list. The job is to decide which names are the same idea in different mouths, and to be honest that deciding is a judgment you are making, not a vote you are counting.

We set Nathan’s sort aside first (six group names, a coherent sequence scheme), leaving 38 names from seven sorts. Two of those were explicit leftover piles — Margie’s “Not sure” and Cami’s “Church-y things I should probably know” — which are findings about the deck and the vocabulary, not groups, and were also excluded. That leaves 36 participant groups to standardize, and they went into seven buckets.

Discussion

What the results mean, including the parts that do not flatter the study.

The mismatch is about grammar, not disagreement

It would be easy and wrong to read this study as “teachers reject the manual’s structure.” Nobody rejected anything. Every card was accepted as a sensible thing a teacher could do, and Curtis — the one participant who knows the manual well — grouped almost along its lines. The mismatch is grammatical. The manual’s five sections are nouns describing what a skill is about. The piles these teachers built are clauses describing when you would need one. Both structures are true of the same content. Only one of them matches the sentence in a teacher’s head on Saturday night, which is never “I would like to study inviting diligent learning” and is always some version of “they won’t talk and I have twenty-five minutes.”

That is an argument for adding a door, not for demolishing the building. The manual’s hierarchy is the right structure for someone browsing to improve; it is the wrong structure for someone arriving with a problem. The live site ships both, and study 3, the tree test is where we tested whether that was a cop-out or a correct reading.

The deck leaked, in exactly one place

“Keeping it about Christ” appeared in all seven folded sorts, making it one of the three universal groups. It is also the one group whose cards we could not de-brand: four of the thirty cards say “the Savior,” because a card from Focus on Jesus Christ that does not mention Christ is a different skill. So the deck telegraphed one of the five section names, and the corresponding pile then formed everywhere.

How much should that discount the finding? Some, honestly — but less than it first appears, for two reasons. First, the piles those four cards formed were not the manual’s section: cards 2 and 4 scattered away from it (card 2 is our first splitter), and cards from three other sections joined it. Second, the finding under test was never “is Christ a category?” It was “do teachers build the manual’s five sections?” and the answer is no in every sort, including all seven where the Christ pile appeared. A leak that produces one group out of seven does not manufacture a scheme mismatch across the other six.

Worth writing on the wall anyway: the strongest result in a card sort is often the one your deck built. Go and look for it before someone else does.

The time pile was a judgment, not a vote

SPECULATION Rests on guessed reception of the schedule change

Five of eight sorts produced a pile about time. On a naive reading that makes Time a category — it out-scored “Feeling the Spirit” and “Hard questions,” which appeared in three folded sorts each and both shipped as categories. We folded it into “Getting ready (and getting through)” instead, and demoted the specific complaint to a problem statement inside that category. Here is the reasoning, because this is the decision on this page a student is most entitled to argue with.

  • A card sort is not a referendum.

    Frequency across eight sorts is evidence about what is salient, not an instruction about what to build. Spencer is blunt about this and she is right: you are the designer; the sort informs the decision and does not make it.

  • Time is not a kind of teaching problem. It is the condition every teaching problem happens inside.

    Every card in every time pile was also plausibly in another pile — that is why the piles kept being built out of borrowed cards. A category whose members all belong somewhere else is a facet wearing a category’s coat. Promoting it would have meant a top-level bucket that competes with all six of its neighbours for every skill in the site.

  • It was the most schedule-contingent signal in the study.

    Four of the five time piles came from the four teachers whose Sunday had just changed, ten weeks earlier. Building a permanent top-level category out of a ten-week-old disruption is how an information architecture acquires a wing named after a season.

  • And it is genuinely a real, permanent worry — so it had to go somewhere visible.

    Margie’s timeless “Getting through it” is the proof that this is not only a September problem. That is why it is a first-class problem statement, not a footnote.

On the live site the resolution is visible and checkable: the category is “Preparing yourself and managing the lesson”, and inside it sits the problem “I run out of time and rush to cover everything” — real, in assets/problems-data.js under prepare › out-of-time, mapping to three skills, one of which is our splitter card 15.

The honest cost of that call: the shipped category title lost the teachers’ own words. “Getting through it” is what these people say; “managing the lesson” is what a designer says. The phrase survives one level down, in the problem statement, which is better than nothing and worse than a category named the way its users would name it. That trade is unresolved, and it is the first thing the nav prototype below is built to interrogate.

Seven groups became seven categories, which should make you suspicious

The standardization produced seven groups and the shipped site has seven categories, one-to-one. Real card sorts are almost never that tidy, and you should know exactly why this one is: this study was written backwards from a decision that had already been made. The categories were committed on 2 June 2026 at e2bf223; the study is set in November 2026. The fiction is constructed to be the study that would have produced the real result, which is the whole method of this companion and also its honest limit. Invented participants cannot surprise you. Real ones would have produced eight groups, or five, or one that nobody on the design team had thought of, and the argument about what to do with it would have been the most valuable meeting of the project.

Two signals we shipped on thinner evidence than we like

“Feeling the Spirit” and “Hard questions” each appeared in three of seven folded sorts. Both became categories anyway, and the reason is not in this study: both are heavily represented in the content inventory, and a category set that cannot hold a chunk of the content is not an option. That is a legitimate reason and it is worth naming out loud, because it is a place where content structure overrode user structure, in a study whose headline finding is that you should not let content structure override user structure.

Cami’s pile is a finding we did not act on

The largest single pile any participant built was Cami Sprague’s seven cards of “Church-y things I should probably know” — the newest teacher in the roster, eight months in the ward, unable to decode almost a quarter of the deck. We treated that as noise about our card wording. It might equally be a finding about the site: that the vocabulary a brand-new teacher lacks is a barrier no reorganization fixes. That thread is picked up, from the outside, in study 5, the external review.

The first artifact from this study

A card sort ends with labels, and labels are promises. “Getting ready (and getting through)” made sense to a teacher holding a pile of cards she had just built herself. Whether it makes sense as one of seven links on a page she has never seen, in three seconds, on a phone, is a completely different question — and it is one this method cannot answer.

So the first thing we built was not a specification. It was a deliberately ugly navigation prototype: the seven standardized labels as seven links, on a home page with nothing else on it. No colour, no brand, no illustrations, no explanatory blurbs, no search box. Everything that could help a reader guess correctly for the wrong reason has been removed, so that the only thing being tested is the words. Behind each label is one plain page listing the problem statements filed under it, so a click can be scored right or wrong.

Two different decks, two different numbers — keep them apart. The 30 cards above are skill cards, drawn from the manual’s 53-skill inventory: they are the study’s input, the content people sorted. The 29 problem statements are a later and different artifact: they are the study’s output, written afterwards in participants’ own grammar and shipped in assets/problems-data.js. The prototype distributes all twenty-nine of them across the seven standardized labels. No card became a statement, and no statement is a card.

What it is for: a first-click test

The question is narrow and answerable: given a teaching problem in a teacher’s own words, does the label invite the right first click? First-click testing rests on a well-replicated finding — when the first click is right, the task usually succeeds; when it is wrong, it usually does not, because people rarely reverse cleanly out of a wrong branch.

Two labels are on trial in particular. “Getting ready (and getting through)” — does the parenthesis do any work, or does a teacher who is out of time on Saturday night scan past it? And “Making it stick” — the one standardized label with no obvious symptom in it, folded from five different phrasings, and therefore the one most likely to be a designer’s word wearing a participant’s coat.

Two administration notes, because a first-click test is sensitive to things that look like housekeeping. The seven labels are ordered to match the live site’s category order, not by how many sorts produced them — ranking the stimulus by our own findings would put the strongest label first and quietly buy the result we are testing for. And the marking banner at the top of every prototype page is scaffolding for readers of this companion, not part of the instrument: it would be removed before the prototype is put in front of a participant, since two live links above the seven targets is exactly the kind of contamination the test is built to avoid.

Open the navigation prototype →  —  seven labels, one page, no styling worth the name. The tasks it will be tested with, and what happened, belong to study 3, the tree test and study 4, the task test.

What we’d do differently

  • Fix card 4, which lost its meaning along with its label-word.

    The manual’s skill is “help students have a sensory experience with scriptural objects that symbolize Jesus Christ.” Our card says “Bring something they can hold.” We stripped the taxonomy and the content in the same stroke, and then watched the card behave like a generic keep-them-busy activity, which is not the skill. De-branding a card is a rewrite with a blast radius; check every one against its source for meaning, not just for leaked vocabulary.

  • Pair people who do not already agree.

    The Beckstrands were the wrong pair for the technique — two co-teachers of one class share a model and so have nothing to negotiate. Pair a Primary teacher with a Relief Society teacher and the boundary arguments become the transcript. Jenae and Aubrey, who teach the same class in alternate weeks, were closer to right and still nearly too aligned.

  • Record the sessions.

    Hand notes caught the loud moments and lost the quiet ones. We have a note reading “~10s, moved hand twice” and no idea what she said in the second half of the pause. Audio is cheap; a second observer is cheaper than a lost finding.

  • Run a closed sort on the seven labels, and run it before shipping.

    We standardized forty-four names into seven by judgment, and then shipped seven category titles that are not the seven standardized names. Two of those renamings — “Getting ready (and getting through)” to “Preparing yourself and managing the lesson,” and “Making it stick” to “Making it relevant and leading to real change” — moved the label away from the participants’ words and toward ours. A twenty- minute closed sort would have told us whether the shipped titles still attract the same cards. We built the nav prototype instead, which answers a narrower question later.

  • Sample the whole inventory, or say what the sample cost.

    Thirty of 53 skills were sorted; twenty-three were never seen by a participant. The seven categories were then built to hold all fifty-three. That is a real extrapolation and we made it quietly. A second deck of the remaining twenty-three, sorted into the seven standardized groups by two of the same participants, would have cost an hour and closed the gap.

  • Take Cami’s pile seriously the first time.

    Seven undecodable cards out of thirty, from the newest teacher, was filed as a card-wording problem. It should have opened a question about who the site assumes its reader is. It took an outside reviewer in study 5, the external review to say it in a way we heard.

  • Ask Primary.

    SPECULATION Four of our eight sorts were Primary teachers who have been teaching a short lesson inside a longer weekly block for their entire callings, and whose piles contained almost none of the anxiety the adult teachers brought. We used that as a control. We never once asked them how they do it. That is a study this companion has not run and probably should.
