This page shows the card-sort assignment the way your grader sees it: as a turn-in scored against the course rubric. Below are two example turn-ins for the same assignment on the same site — one that earns full marks (60/60) and one that earns 31/60. After each section, the red box is the grader’s pen: the score, and why.

Scope note

Real: the assignment text, the rubric and its point bands, the Teacher Development Skills manual, the 21 card texts (each a skill name quoted verbatim — see the appendix in turn-in A), and the design direction turn-in A lands on, which shipped in the live site.

The assignment (real)

What to turn in — a single PDF containing:

  1. 1. Names of the people who were in your card-sorting study.
  2. 2. Pictures of the resulting card sorts for each participant.
  3. 3. One page or less of analysis: trends you saw in the sorts, and specific isolated incidents.
  4. 4. One page or less on the categories and labels you ended up with, and a justification based on the results and analysis. Don’t overthink this.
  5. 5. Half a page or less on what you saw that you are going to ignore — for now and maybe forever. This is to get you thinking critically about your own results.

The rubric (real)

Three criteria, banded points. Notice the weighting escalates from procedure to judgment: converting findings into design commitments is 42% of the grade.

CriterionFull marksDevelopingBeginning
Methods 15 — required participant count; standard method, at most minor errors 10 — more than half the participants; several minor errors, maybe one significant 5 — half or fewer; sloppy, significant protocol errors
Results & Analysis 20 — concise results; synthesis across participants; calibrated claims; raw data as an appendix 13 — accurate but participant-by-participant, OR over-claims. Over-claiming caps you here regardless of polish. 7 — disorganized, findings buried, no real analysis, raw data missing
Design Decisions 25 — every decision cites its finding; mostly concrete; “keep” decisions count fully 17 — reasonable but the reader must infer which result motivated what; some decisions vague 8 — decisions ignore or predate the data, or no decisions — just restated findings

This study requires five participants. No marks (0) on any criterion means the element simply isn’t there.

Grader · Raw data feeds Results & Analysis

All five sorts shown in full, titles verbatim, every card traceable to a real skill id through the appendix. This is the raw-data appendix the Results & Analysis criterion requires — without it, that criterion caps at Developing no matter how good the writing is. Points land with element 3.

Element 3Analysis (one page)

Trends. Nobody rebuilt the manual’s five sections: 28 group titles across five sorts, zero section names. Groups formed around situations, not subjects — 17 of 28 titles name a moment teaching goes a particular way (“Getting them to talk”, “Running out of time”, “When it gets hard”). One situation appears on every sort under five different names — do I know these people — and the same four cards (012.i2, 013.i3, 014.3, 015.1) move as a block on four of five sorts. Three sorts partly organized by when a skill is used (before Sunday / during / after), an axis the manual does not have.

A trend we don’t trust. Every sort has a Christ group, but five cards contain the words “Jesus Christ” and one participant titled his group “Christ (all of these say Christ)” — he sorted on the string, not the meaning.

Isolated incidents. P13, the only Primary teacher, built a pile meaning “not for my class” and left four cards in it (once). P05 stalled on 006.2 — “sensory experience with scriptural objects” is nobody’s hallway vocabulary (once). And P06 asked whether a card could go two places (once):

“I can put it under time or under whether they got it. It’s the same thing I’d do. Why do I have to pick?”

INVENTED QUOTE P06, on card 018.1

Grader · Results & Analysis 20 / 20 — Full marks

This is synthesis, not recap: claims are counted across participants (“17 of 28 titles”, “four of five sorts”), and trends are separated from one-time incidents with the frequency stated. The claims are calibrated — the one trend the data can’t support (the Christ pile) is flagged as suspect instead of banked. Raw data is in the appendix. Compare turn-in B, which has the same ingredients and does none of this.

Element 4Categories and labels, with justification (one page)

  1. Organize by teaching situation, not by the manual’s sections. Because 17 of 28 titles named a situation and zero named a section, the site’s primary navigation becomes problem categories.
  2. Label categories in the teacher’s own words. Because every title was hallway language (nobody typed “skill” or “doctrine”), labels will be first-person sentences about trouble — e.g. “No one answers when I ask a question” — not topics.
  3. A “knowing my class” category is non-negotiable. Because the same four-card block appeared on all five sorts under five names, it gets its own category regardless of how the rest shakes out.
  4. Let one skill live under several categories. Because 018.1 landed in time-shaped groups on two sorts and assessment-shaped groups on two others (and P06 asked why she had to pick), the mapping is many-to-many.
  5. Keep the manual’s five-section browse as a second path. Because two sorts still held subject-shaped groups, the hierarchy stays alongside the problem categories — unchanged.
  6. Directional: revisit card 006.2’s wording wherever it surfaces in the site. One stall (P05) isn’t enough to dictate the fix, only to flag the phrase.

Grader · Design Decisions 25 / 25 — Full marks

Every decision opens with “because” and a counted finding — the reader never has to guess which result motivated what. Decision 5 is an evidence-backed decision to keep something, which counts fully. Decision 6 is directional and says so; that’s fine where the data flags a problem without dictating the fix. Six decisions, no padding — volume earns nothing.

(Companion note, outside the fiction: decisions 1–4 are the real shipped direction — the seven problem categories of problems.html, commit e2bf223.)

Element 5What we’re ignoring (half page)

  • The Christ pile, as evidence about grouping. Deck artifact: five cards share the words, and one sorter told us in his title that the words did the sorting. A Christ-centered category may still ship, but it has to earn its place from the manual’s own emphasis — not from this pile.
  • Differences in group counts (4 vs 7). Tool-and-device artifact: the iPad sorter stopped splitting piles when dragging got tiresome. We compare what went together, never how many piles.
  • What sat in the leftover piles. We dealt the deck in the same order every session, so the leftovers track fatigue and deal order, not the skills. (Next time: shuffle.)

Grader · Element 5 no separate points — confirms the bands above

This element carries no rubric points of its own, but it’s where calibration becomes visible: each ignored finding names the artifact that produced it. This is why element 3 could claim full marks — the most quotable finding in the study (every sort had a Christ group!) was spent zero times. Weak submissions ignore findings silently; strong ones ignore them in writing.

Turn-in B — a weak submission (31/60)

FICTITIOUS DATA A second invented submission — same assignment, same deck, a different (invented) run of the study. The participants are ward-roster members P14 Bryce Tuckett, P03 Sione Latu, and P15 Cami Sprague. Every error below is constructed on purpose.

Example turn-in B · submitted as a single PDF

Element 1Participants

We did our card sort with Bryce, Sione, and Cami from the ward. We got them all together after ward council on June 14 so we could do everyone in one meeting, and to save time we set up four starter groups in the tool (Doctrine, Preparation, Discussion, Ministering) that they could use or change.

Grader · Methods 10 / 15 — Developing

Three of the five required participants — more than half, so this isn’t the bottom band, but the count alone rules out full marks. “Starter groups” is the significant error: supplying categories turns an open sort into a half-closed one, and you can see the damage in element 2 — both boards echo your four titles. Sorting everyone in one room lets participants influence each other (minor). Also: who are these people? No callings, no classes — a reader can’t judge whether the sample resembles the users.

Element 2Pictures of the sorts

Bryce’s and Cami’s boards are below. Sione’s board didn’t save before the tool’s free session expired, but his groups were similar to Bryce’s.

Grader · Raw data feeds Results & Analysis

Two boards for three participants: a third of the raw data is gone, and “his groups were similar” is not data. Look at what the boards actually show: eight of ten group titles are the four titles you typed into the tool yourself, and on both boards the Christ-language cards ended up stranded in the leftover pile because your starter categories gave them nowhere to live. That stranding is the most interesting finding in this study — watch what happens to it.

Element 3Analysis

Bryce made five groups and used all four of our categories, and put four cards in Misc. Sione talked through his groups and mostly matched Bryce. Cami also made five groups and hers were close to Bryce’s too, with a bigger Other pile. Everyone agreed the manual’s structure is confusing, and teachers universally want categories like Doctrine, Preparation, Discussion, and Ministering. Sione said “I’d never look for this stuff on a website anyway,” but he was probably joking.

Grader · Results & Analysis 13 / 20 — Developing

This is a session-by-session recap, not analysis: nothing is counted across participants, and “close to Bryce’s” hides the actual disagreements (compare where 018.1 and 007.2 sit on the two boards). It also over-claims twice — “everyone agreed the manual’s structure is confusing” (no participant was shown the manual’s structure) and “teachers universally want” the four categories you handed them. Over-claiming caps this criterion at Developing regardless of polish; the incomplete raw data would have done the same. Not Beginning, because what is reported is accurate and readable.

Element 4Categories and labels

Based on our study, the site’s navigation will be:

  • Doctrine, Preparation, Discussion, Ministering — the four categories our participants used.
  • We will also improve the labels to be clearer for teachers.
  • We will add a search bar so teachers can find skills faster.

Grader · Design Decisions 8 / 25 — Beginning

The four shipped categories are the four starter titles you supplied before anyone touched a card — the sort could not have produced anything else. That is a plan made before the study ran; the study was theater. “Improve the labels” commits to nothing, and nothing in a card sort says anything about search. Meanwhile the one genuine finding your own boards contain — the Christ cards stranded in Misc/Other on both sorts, telling you a category is missing — motivates no decision at all.

Element 5What we’re ignoring

Nothing. We think all of our findings are important, and we plan to use all of them going forward.

Grader · Element 5 no separate points — but it confirms the bands above

A study always produces more results than evidence; “nothing” means you haven’t asked which of your results you actually trust. And this submission did ignore things — Sione’s “I’d never look for this on a website” and the stranded Christ cards — it just did so silently. Ignoring a finding in writing is a skill; ignoring it silently is how the over-claims in element 3 survive to element 4.

What separates 60 from 31

Same assignment, same deck, similar-looking PDFs. The gap is a chain of custody: turn-in A collected data it could trust (five clean solo sorts), made only the claims that data supports (counted across participants, artifacts flagged), and then let every decision cite its finding. Turn-in B broke the chain at the first link — the starter categories — and everything downstream, including its confident tone, inherited the break. Note where the rubric spends its points: the mechanics of running the sort are 15; the judgment afterward is 45.
