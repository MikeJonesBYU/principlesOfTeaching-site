1. The prototype

Link: the semantic build — prototypes/v1/semantic/. It is Team A’s prototype v1 with exactly one subsystem replaced. The data file is not a copy — the page loads ../team-a/v1-data.js itself, so the 53 blocks, the revised hierarchy, both views, and the one block template are Team A’s own, byte for byte. The stylesheet is loaded the same way. What is new: semantic-index.js (a 594 KB precomputed meaning index), the search layer in app.js that reads it, and an “About this search” page inside the prototype that explains the engine to anyone who asks it.

The one-sentence version of the change: the original build answers the words you typed; this build also answers the thing you meant — and it does it with no server, no model in the browser, and no AI call at lookup time. Search is dictionary lookups and addition, and works offline.

2. Design rationale

The information architecture is inherited, not invented — and inherited by reference. The assignment says the hierarchy is the one the tree test produced, and it is: all six committed decisions (D1–D6), the trial “What it feels like” branch, the multi-filing of 006.title_number2, the situation labels, both views. Because the build loads Team A’s data file rather than copying it, the claim “same IA” is not a promise kept by discipline; it is a property of the architecture. There is nothing to drift.

The visual system is inherited the same way, and this report restates it in exact values as the spec requires. Monochromatic on one hue — base 217° — with its complement at 37° as the single accent, neutrals free:

  • --ink       #0F1B2D — body text
  • --blue-900  #12253F — headings
  • --blue-700  #1B3A6B — BASE
  • --blue-500  #2E5C9E — links, active, focus
  • --blue-300  #8AA8D2 — TINT, quiet rules
  • --blue-100  #DCE6F5 — chips, tags, panels
  • --blue-050  #F1F5FB — page ground
  • --shade     #0B1626 — SHADE, shadows only
  • --accent-700 #8F4705 — filled-control hover
  • --accent-600 #B45C07 — filled control & accent text (4.7:1 under white)
  • --accent-500 #D9800F — non-text accent only
  • --paper     #FFFFFF · --gray-600 #57606E · --gray-200 #DDE2E9

The accent discipline survives unchanged: the accent is spent once per screen, routed by body[data-focus], and this build adds no new accent sites. The one visual addition is a small “semantic build” pill beside the wordmark, drawn in currentColor at reduced opacity — it identifies the variant without spending color, because a person comparing the two builds side by side must never wonder which tab is which. The Gestalt grounding is Team A’s: figure-ground from the one raised surface, alignment and proximity from the shared grid.

The search: what the brief asked, and the question this build adds. The brief asks for a literal keyword baseline plus a hand-built, committed concept layer — vocabulary harvested from the card sort and tree test, defensible term by term. The original build does exactly that, and its committed vocabulary is genuinely good: 35 concepts, 175 phrases counting variants, every one traceable to a participant. But a committed vocabulary has a boundary, and the boundary is exactly where a teacher stands at 8 PM on Saturday: “kids are noisy” is not in it. “teenagers on their phones” is not in it. The question this build asks is: what should the search box do with the words nobody wrote down?

The answer is a meaning index, computed once, ahead of time. At build time, a script (tools/build_semantic_index.py) assembles 638 passages — every block’s situation label, its verbatim manual sentence, the sentences of its manual “Define” text, the names of every branch it is filed under — and compares 11,869 candidate words and phrases against all of them with a sentence-embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) running locally. The candidates come from five places: a 9,884-word common-English list; every content word and bigram in the corpus itself; the words participants used in the two studies; the committed concept vocabulary; and a hand-written seed lexicon of classroom-situation words. The 9,121 that turn out to mean something near a skill — 7,812 words, 1,309 phrases — ship as a static lookup table: term → [skill, weight 1–100, passage]. The model never ships. The browser looks things up.

The shaping is stated so it can be argued with. A single word runs cooler against a sentence than a phrase does, so each term length gets its own floor and scale: single words 0.22–0.55 cosine, two-word phrases 0.30–0.68, longer 0.34–0.75; anything under its floor is dropped — that is the whole filter between 11,869 candidates and 9,121 survivors. A term that is close to everything (“students”) carries little information, so scores shrink toward the term’s own mean before the floor is applied. A hit on a branch a block is merely filed under is worth 0.7 of a hit on the block’s own words. Each term keeps its top eight skills.

One kind of content is genuinely new, and it is authored, not harvested. The manual describes teacher actions (“Use pictures and videos…”); teachers search in classroom situations (“kids are noisy”). No amount of embedding bridges text that simply is not there. So each of the 21 branches of the hierarchy — 6 categories, 15 groups — carries one written situation sentence, in teacher language: “The class is bored and restless — phones out, eyes wandering, side conversations starting. You are losing them.” The model generalizes each sentence to words nobody wrote down — “rowdy” lands on it without appearing in it. These sentences are the same design move as the tree test’s D4 situation labels, extended from blocks to branches; but where D4’s labels were justified from participant wording, these are written from my own conviction about how teachers talk. That is a grounding difference, not a footnote, and it is called out again under “what I am ignoring.”

The committed vocabulary stays authoritative. All 175 committed phrases keep their hand-built targets, at weights that echo the original layer’s ordering (a committed block target outranks a committed group target outranks a committed category), and a committed target always beats the model’s opinion of the same phrase. The model only speaks where the studies were silent. The result line says which happened, in words: “our studies mapped ‘was that the spirit’ here” versus “your ‘phones’ is close in meaning to ‘Phones out, eyes wandering…’”.

Ranking is still one addition, and the ordering can still be said out loud. The literal layer is unchanged: label 6, manual sentence 4, branch names 2, moment names 1, word-prefix matching. A semantic hit contributes weight/100 × 6 — so typing a skill’s exact meaning is worth exactly a label match, and a loose synonym is worth less than the manual’s own words, never more. The query is cut into every phrase and word the index knows (longest first; the words inside a known phrase still count, since they point at the same meaning), and each known term adds its list. One tuning change to the literal layer is disclosed: the stoplist grew by the pronouns and question words — “their,” “how,” “was” — that were matching half the manual by prefix and drowning the ranking in noise.

The search is honest about its edges. Words the index does not know are named on the results screen — “Words this search did not recognize: are. They contributed nothing to the ranking” — so a teacher is never left guessing which half of their sentence did the work. The no-results state reports the real size of the vocabulary it searched. And the prototype carries an “About this search” page that explains the whole mechanism — where the meaning comes from, when it was computed, what never leaves the browser — in the same voice as the rest of the site.

Testing: the ranking has a twin, and the twin has a suite. tools/test_semantic_search.py replicates the browser’s scoring math in Python — same stoplist, same weights, same segmentation — against the same shipped index, and runs an 18-query meaning suite: “kids are noisy,” “how do i know it was the spirit,” “shy class,” “follow up during the week,” plus literal regressions that exact wording still wins. 18/18, and the suite ran after every tuning change, which is how two bad ideas (an auto-split of the situation sentences; a template trick for single words) were caught and reverted before they shipped.

Logging: the instrument is inherited, and the two builds cannot contaminate each other. Same schema, same exports, result rows still tied to the query that produced them. The variant logs under its own storage key and exports under its own filename prefix, so a future task test can run either build — or both, between subjects — without one build’s sessions leaking into the other’s data.

Cost, stated plainly. The index is 594 KB — about forty times Team A’s hand-built concept layer — loaded once and cached. Rebuilding it requires Python and one local model on the maintainer’s machine; the deployed site remains static files with zero dependencies. And one rule of the brief is bent, knowingly: the assignment says the search is built with no AI. No AI runs at lookup time — the browser does arithmetic — but a model did run once, at build time, the way a compiler runs. Whether that honors the rule or evades it is the grader’s call, not mine, and the build is designed so the call is easy to make: everything the model decided is inspectable in the shipped index, term by term.

4. What I am ignoring

Antonyms cluster. Embedding models place “noisy” near “the quiet ones” — they live in the same neighborhood of meaning. One situation clause was removed during tuning for exactly this (“noisy” was landing on the quiet-student sentence), but the failure mode is structural and will surface again somewhere else.

Rare single words are weak. The model has almost no useful signal for “wiggly.” The index simply does not contain it, and the results screen will say so, but a teacher who types only that word gets nothing where a human librarian would do fine.

Meaning is added, never composed. The engine sums evidence per term. “Not the teacher talking” scores the same as “the teacher talking” — negation, scope, and word order are invisible to a lookup table. This is the honest ceiling of no-model-at-lookup-time, and I would rather state the ceiling than pretend the table understands syntax.

Nobody has used it. Every claim above is structural or self-tested. Whether meaning-matching actually helps a teacher at 8 PM on Saturday — or whether the why-lines just add reading — is an empirical question, and the five-user task test that could answer it belongs to the graded chain, not to this exploration.

Grader · 1. Met the functional spec · 18/18 — Skilled

I went looking for the seam between the two builds and found exactly one, which is the one advertised.

  • Every required behavior works. Both hierarchy views, the multi-filed block, search from every screen, facet narrowing, the designed no-results state, the log exporting in both formats — and the whole thing still works with the network cable pulled, which is the strongest possible reading of “no server.” “The prototype meets every functional requirement in the assignment spec, and the functionality works reliably.”
  • The twin test harness is the detail I will show other teams. A second implementation of the ranking math, against the same shipped index, with an 18-query suite that ran on every tuning change. That is the difference between “it seemed better” and “it measurably stopped being worse” — a prototype whose search can be regression tested is an instrument, not a demo.
  • The two builds cannot contaminate each other’s logs. Separate storage keys, separate export prefixes. Someone was thinking about the study this artifact exists to serve.

Full marks, without an argument.

Grader · 2. Met the visual design spec · 12/13 — Skilled, one off

Inheriting a visual system is a legitimate design decision when the point of the build is a controlled comparison, and the report restates the system in exact values rather than waving at it. The pill is the right move: identification without spending color.

  • The accent discipline survives contact with a new feature. The semantic layer added result annotations, a meta line, and a whole explanatory page, and none of them spent the accent. That is harder than it sounds.
  • But the why-lines cost you a point. On a phone, a result item is now a situation label, a verbatim manual sentence, moment tags, and a quoted why-line that wraps to three lines — the longest element in the card, set in the smallest type. The eye should land on the block title first; on narrow screens it lands in the middle of the quote. “Visual hierarchy clearly supports the user’s goals” is the band language, and the why-line works against it exactly where this site will actually be used. Truncate it, or reveal it on demand.

Grader · 3. Met the information architecture spec · 13/13 — Skilled

The IA is inherited by reference, which is the strongest form of “inherited, not invented” this assignment has seen.

  • Loading the other team’s data file is the architectural argument of the course, made physical. Single source, multiple views now includes: two builds. The hierarchy cannot drift between them because there is nothing to drift — and the report’s own outline renders from that same file, so the required element cannot disagree with the artifact either. “The hierarchy reflects deliberate design decisions” — here the deliberate decision is that there is exactly one of it.
  • Search results remain a selection over the single source — a third door into the same 53 blocks, with the facet chips still computed from the result set on screen.

Top band.

Grader · 4. Grounded in the previous study · 14/16 — Skilled, two held back

The anchor design is the best answer I have seen to “how do you add machinery without orphaning your evidence”: everything the studies committed keeps its hand-built targets, the model is only allowed to speak where the studies were silent, and the result line tells the user which voice they are hearing. That is grounding taken seriously. Two points are held back anyway, and I want the reasons on the record because they are the teaching content of this page.

  • The situation sentences are conviction, not evidence. The report says so itself, plainly, and D4 gives the move a pedigree — but D4’s labels were defended from participant wording and these 21 sentences are defended from the author’s ear. They are load-bearing: most of the headline queries land because of them. A team that ships load-bearing vocabulary should harvest it, or test it. “Demonstrably grounded in the needs of your users” is the band; “plausibly grounded in the instructor’s model of the users” is what these sentences are.
  • The no-AI rule was bent, and disclosure is not the same as compliance. The compiler argument is a good argument — nothing intelligent runs at lookup time, and every model opinion is inspectable in the shipped index. I accept it for an instructor exploration. I would not accept it silently from a team, and the point held back here is what makes that precedent legible: read the brief as written, and when you decide to argue with it, price the argument in.

This is what a near-miss looks like when the work is excellent: both deductions are about provenance, not quality.

Why not 60

Both deductions are provenance, not craft — and that is the lesson worth the three points. The build’s cleverest machinery (the situation sentences, the meaning index) is exactly the machinery that is hardest to ground in a study, because it was invented to cover the places the studies never reached. When your best feature outruns your evidence, the rubric does not ask you to slow down; it asks you to say so — this report does — and then it prices the distance. A perfect score here would have required either harvesting the 21 situation sentences from participants, or running the brief as written and building without the model. Both were available. Neither was chosen, for stated reasons. 57/60 is what stated reasons buy.
