The Jyväskylä Finland Stake, imagined

Jyväskylä is said YEW-vass-kew-la: stress on the first syllable, the y is the ü in German über, and the ä is the a in cat. Every name on this page has its own guide.

The Timpanogos Shadows Ward is one kind of place to teach a gospel class: a big, settled Utah ward where nobody drives far and everyone already knows the words. This page imagines a very different one — the stake that covers the northern and eastern two-thirds of Finland, from the lakes around Jyväskylä to Rovaniemi on the Arctic Circle — so the card sort can be run again with different people and the same cards. It lets us ask the question every card-sort report ought to ask itself: how much of what we found belongs to the people we asked?

Like the ward page, this is a reference sheet. When the Finnish study says “F03,” it means the person listed under F03 here, and it always will. The IDs start with F so they can never be confused with the ward’s P01–P26.

Scope note: a real stake, invented people

FICTITIOUS DATA

The stake is real. The people on this page are not. The Jyväskylä Finland Stake was organized in May 2021 and takes in the congregations of central, eastern, and northern Finland (sources at the bottom of the page). Nobody on this page belongs to it, because nobody on this page exists. The twelve people, their callings, families, jobs, and phones, and every word in quotation marks, were made up entirely by AI for this course. None of them is based on a real person: not a member of the real stake, not anyone known to Professor Mike Jones, not anyone else. Their names were picked from among the commonest in each language so that none of them points at anyone.

We did not invent the stake’s own numbers. The ward page has a table of invented ward figures. This page does not, because a real stake has real numbers — membership, attendance, how many teachers it calls — and they are not ours to make up. Every number here about Finland, the Church, or the stake is a published figure, with its source; the numbers attached to the invented people are as invented as they are. Where the page describes the stake’s congregations in general terms (how big a class is, who sits in it), that description is stamped SPECULATION. For the same reason, no invented person here holds a leadership calling in a real unit. They teach classes, or they sit in them.

Speculation note: this page guesses twice

SPECULATION Written September 2026 · set November 2026

It guesses about the future. Like the ward page, it is set ten weeks into the new Sunday schedule: sacrament meeting, then twenty-five minutes of Sunday School every week, then twenty-five minutes of elders quorum, Relief Society, Young Women, or an Aaronic Priesthood quorum every week, with Primary for fifty-five minutes alongside. That schedule is real. It began in congregations worldwide on 6 September 2026. This page was written that same month, so it describes November before November happened.

It guesses about people it has never met, and that is the bigger guess. The ward page’s findings encode what the instructor believes about teachers he has spent years around. Nothing on this page has that backing. It was built from published sources — Church statistics, Finnish census and education figures, research on Finnish communication — plus reasoning about how those facts might show up in a Sunday classroom. No Finnish Latter-day Saint was asked. Every sentence about how people in this stake think, feel, or teach is a hypothesis. Read the page as the starting point for a real study, because only a real study could tell you whether any of it is true.

How to say the names

Finnish spelling is almost perfectly regular, so a handful of rules will carry you through every name on this page.

  • Stress is always on the first syllable.

    JUK-ka, never juk-KA. KA-jaa-ni, never ka-JAA-ni.

  • j is an English y.

    Jukka is YOOK-kah; Kajaani is KAH-yah-nee.

  • Three vowels English does not have.

    ä is the a in cat. ö is the vowel in fur, without the r. y is German ü: say ee with your lips rounded.

  • Double letters are held longer, and it matters.

    The kk in Pekka and Jukka is a real pause on the k; the ii in Liisa is a long ee.

  • Vowel pairs glide in one syllable, and every letter is sounded.

    ai as in eye, ei as in eight. uo glides from oo to o, close to the wo in woke, so the guides write it WOH. ie glides from ee to e, close to the ye in yes, so the guides write it YEH. A long ee, as in Eero, is a pure e held long, not the ay of eight. No letter is silent, including a final e.

  • The places.

    Jyväskylä YEW-vass-kew-la · Oulu OH-loo · Kuopio KWOH-pee-oh · Kajaani KAH-yah-nee · Rovaniemi ROH-vah-nyeh-mee · Sodankylä SOH-dahn-kew-la · Espoo ESS-poh

Stake geography and rhythm

What is real. The stake was organized on 16 May 2021 from three districts (Oulu, Pietarsaari, and Kuopio) and two wards (Jyväskylä and Lappeenranta). It has twelve congregations, and the people on this page are imagined in five of the places it reaches: Jyväskylä, Kuopio, Oulu, Kajaani, and Rovaniemi. By area it is the largest stake in Finland. The distances are real too. Jyväskylä to Kuopio is about 150 kilometres, and Jyväskylä to Oulu about 340. Oulu to Rovaniemi is about 220 more, and Rovaniemi to Sodankylä 130 more again. The Helsinki Finland Temple, in Espoo, is three hours’ drive from Jyväskylä and more than eight hundred kilometres from Rovaniemi. In late November Jyväskylä gets about six hours of daylight, Oulu about five, and Rovaniemi less than that. Winter tyres are compulsory from December, and the roads can be icy from October to April.

SPECULATION How we imagine a Sunday here

In the congregations these people attend, a Sunday School class is often a handful of people. A Primary class may hold every child from four to ten. In some rooms someone is quietly interpreting at the side, and in others nobody is, and the newest members follow as best they can. When the roads are bad, a family that lives two hours from its branch joins the classes on a laptop. (The Church’s handbook lets local leaders authorize remote participation in some meetings; this page imagines one invented family’s arrangement, not the practice of any real branch.)

The five-minute transition barely exists in a small branch, where the Sunday School class and the Relief Society are the same eight women, twenty-five minutes apart, on the same chairs. Stake conference is a six-hour drive for some. The temple is a stake bus trip, planned months ahead. Weekly Sunday School in a branch with two teachers means your turn comes every other week.

The teaching corps, as we imagine it

SPECULATION A general picture reasoned from the facts above, not a description of the real stake’s teachers, whom we have not met.

  • Classes are small, and mixed.

    One Primary class for every child from four to ten. One youth Sunday School class for everyone from twelve to seventeen. One adult class, with the same dozen people in it every week. A class of three is not a failure here; it is simply how many people there are.

  • More than one language in the room.

    Finnish first. English as the bridge nobody grew up speaking but most people can use. And a few members learning Finnish — from Ukraine, the Philippines, West Africa — who follow a Finnish lesson at the speed of their vocabulary.

  • Some are trained teachers; most are not.

    In a country where a classroom teacher holds a master’s degree, a few people in every congregation teach for a living, and they may use the vocabulary of that profession without embarrassment. Many adults are converts who learned the gospel from missionaries and manuals.

  • Silence may mean something different.

    A well-known 1985 essay by two Finnish linguists, “The Silent Finn,” described long conversational pauses as ordinary in Finland rather than awkward. Finnish communication researchers have argued ever since about how much of that is myth. A teacher raised with it might hear a twenty-second silence as thinking rather than failure. Whether that happens in a Sunday class is exactly the kind of question a study should check rather than assume.

  • Faith may be more private than performed.

    Finland is often described as a country of “belonging without believing,” where religion is a private matter. Converts carry that culture into a church that asks them to bear testimony aloud. How that shows up on a Sunday, if it does at all, is a hypothesis this page cannot settle.

  • Devices: the phone, and for some a laptop on Sunday.

    Gospel Library in Finnish on a phone, the manual as the Finnish edition of Teaching in the Savior’s Way, and in the far-flung families the laptop that carries the video link.

Ten weeks in

SPECULATION What the new schedule might have done to a small, spread-out stake between September and November — guessed in September.

  • Twenty-five minutes with three people is a different clock.

    In a class of three a real discussion can finish. The Utah ward’s twenty-five-minute wall is less a wall here than a frame.

  • Weekly classes, with the same few teachers.

    A branch with two Sunday School teachers now gives each of them a turn every other week. It is the same doubling the Utah ward’s Relief Society feels, arriving everywhere at once.

  • Two windows instead of one.

    For a family on a video link, the second hour is now two twenty-five-minute classes, and the connection has to hold for both.

  • What got better.

    A weekly class means that for the first time, a thread started one Sunday is still warm the next. And the monthly teacher council, held during a class period, is the first time some branches’ teachers have sat in a room together to talk about teaching — even when the room holds two people.

Aino Karjalainen

63 · baptized 1984 · class teacher, grades 1–6, 37 years in Jyväskylä schools, retired 2025 · iPad, and a ring binder she has kept since 1991

Trained as a class teacher at the University of Jyväskylä in the early eighties, in the city where Finland’s first Finnish-language teacher college opened in 1863 — a fact she will mention once, drily. What she worries about on Sunday is the one thing her training never covered: of the twelve women in front of her, three are still new to Finnish, and she cannot tell whose silence she is waiting through.

“A silence in a room of Finnish ten-year-olds I can read like a book. A silence in Relief Society has three languages in it.”

What they might say

  • “It went quite well.” (close to the highest praise available)
  • “It wasn’t bad.” (higher)
  • “Nobody said anything, but it was a good silence.”
  • “Give them a moment. They’re thinking.”
  • “I’ll read the question again.”
  • “Did the connection hold?”
  • “Could you say that in English? Slower?”
  • “It’s my turn again already.”
  • “Let’s sing the second verse too.”
  • “Who’s bringing the laptop?”

What they might rarely say in class

  • “Let’s go around the room and everyone share.”
  • “I felt the Spirit so strongly.” (of themselves, out loud, in a class)
  • “I know with every fibre of my being.” (an idiom that does not translate)
  • “That was awesome!”
  • Anyone’s name, called on without warning

And one difference from the Utah ward runs the other way. For the trained teachers, words like wait time, interaction, and learning are ordinary. They are the vocabulary of a respected profession, and nobody here is embarrassed to use it.

A few more habits of speech

  • One word with no English partner.

    The Lutheran church’s word for turning toward God in prayer and quiet is hiljentyminen, literally “becoming quiet.” A member raised Lutheran might reach for it for the stillness at the start of a meeting. English gives you either “stillness” or “devotion,” and each is half of the word.

  • Understatement is the register.

    “Quite good” is warm, and “not bad” is warmer. An American superlative translated literally sounds either sarcastic or like a sales pitch.

  • English is the bridge, and nobody’s first language.

    In a room of Finns, a Filipino nurse, a Ukrainian chemist, and a Ghanaian researcher, English is the language nobody’s mother spoke and nearly everybody can use. That makes a lesson taught in it plainer, and a joke told in it riskier.

  • “The app” and “the manual.”

    As in Utah, “the app” is Gospel Library — here in Finnish — and “the manual” is the Finnish edition of Teaching in the Savior’s Way. The skills the card sort uses come from a manual we could not find in Finnish, which is its own small finding about the site the studies examine.

How the studies use this page

  • IDs are permanent, and they are F-numbers.

    A study in this setting cites participants as F01…F12. IDs are never reassigned, and a person’s calling, congregation, device, and personality stay fixed. New people join at the end of the list. The ward’s P-numbers and these F-numbers never mix.

  • A real stake is handled differently from an invented ward.

    No invented person holds a leadership calling in a real unit. No stake statistic is invented. Every general claim about the stake’s congregations carries a SPECULATION stamp.

  • The talk section is source material, not findings.

    It records guesses about how these people might talk. What a study does with the people — what they sorted, what they wrote on their piles — belongs to the study.
