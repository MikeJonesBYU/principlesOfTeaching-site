Recruiting. Nine people, eight sorts, from five congregations: Jyväskylä, Oulu, Kuopio, Kajaani, and Rovaniemi. Most teachers in this stake don’t live near the stake centre, and we wanted the ones who don’t. One of us grew up in the Oulu Ward. Her mother passed our invitation to teachers she knew in the other congregations, and we took everyone who said yes, up to nine. We know that recruiting through one family’s friendships leans toward people who answer messages from someone’s mother.

Protocol. Open sort, no starter categories, no hints, and the same instructions for everyone. We couldn’t sit at anyone’s table, so ten days ahead we mailed each participant a printed deck in a sealed envelope with sticky notes and a pen. On a video call they opened it, sorted on their own table with the phone camera pointed down, named each pile on a sticky note in their own words, and photographed the finished sort before touching anything. The teammate from Oulu ran every session. She ran five in Finnish, and three in English for the people who chose the English deck. We kept notes on what people said while they hesitated.

Three deviations, and what they cost. The deck. We reused, card for card, the 24-card deck another team in our section built for a Utah ward, so the two populations could be compared. That means we also inherit its deviation from Spencer’s 30–60 cards, and its cost: thinner patterns. Translation. The deck needed a Finnish edition. We translated the 24 short handles ourselves, and took each card’s full sentence from the Church’s Finnish materials where we could find it and from our own translation where we couldn’t. Participants chose their deck, and three chose English. For this report we translated the five Finnish sorts’ pile labels into English. A second translator, a Finnish returned missionary at BYU, did the same independently. The labels where we disagreed are flagged below. Remote sessions. We saw hands and cards, not faces, so we surely missed hesitations. Liisa’s connection from Sodankylä dropped twice. Both times she kept sorting, and we picked up again from her photo.

Translation notes — the three words where our two translators disagreed (the first appears in two sorts):

  • “Devotional stillness” (S1) and “Stillness” (S7). Both wrote the word Finnish Lutherans use for turning to God in prayer and quiet. It means “becoming still” and “turning to prayer” at once. One translator wrote stillness and the other devotion, and each is half right.
  • “Reading the silence” (S2). The Finnish is closer to “interpreting the quiet.” We kept the more natural English.
  • “What we don’t easily say aloud” (S2). The Finnish verb is gentler than can’t and stronger than don’t. Easily is our compromise.

3. Analysis: trends and isolated incidents

Trends

1. Nobody sorted by the manual. No sort reproduced the manual’s five sections, and no one tried. Three sorts organized around situations in the sorter’s own class (S2, S3, S8), and one around a trained teacher’s categories (S1). One sorted by where the class happens (S5) and one by the parts of a lesson (S6). One followed a different official structure, the way missionaries are taught to teach (S4). The pair sorted around stillness and what is hard to say (S7).

2. Everyone agreed on one cluster, and nobody named it the same way. C11, C12, and C21 (check learning, listen and follow up, resist answering everything yourself) were in the same pile in all eight sorts. C14 (pause before responding) joined them in six, and both times it left, it went to a pile about the Spirit or stillness (S4, S7). Now look at the labels. The three sorts that named the pile after listening, silence, or waiting were all by Finnish-born sorters: “Wait time and listening,” “Reading the silence,” “Asking and waiting.” The two who named it after getting people to talk grew up elsewhere and sorted the English deck: “Getting them to open up,” “Getting discussion going.” That is five sorts, and deck language and birthplace are tangled together in them, so we are not calling this a cultural finding. We are calling it a labeling finding. To some teachers these cards mean “make room”; to others they mean “get them talking.” A label that says only one of those will lose the other half.

3. A Spirit pile, made of quiet rather than of the word “Spirit.” Four of eight sorts made a pile about the Spirit or about devotional stillness, and all four contain C08 (pray to know how to help them) and C13 (sacred music). Two used the word Spirit (Eero’s “Teaching by the Spirit,” Samuel’s “Spirit and testimony”). Two never did (Aino’s “Devotional stillness,” Tuula and Pekka’s “Stillness”). C20 (ponder a personal circumstance) joined both stillness piles; for these two sorts, pondering is a quiet thing. Tuula explained why music belongs there with a generalization we record as hers, not ours: “Finns will sing what they would never say.” INVENTED QUOTE What we take from it is narrower: in four sorts, sacred music sat with prayer, not with openers.

4. Testimony left the Savior. The Savior pile itself is solid. C01 and C05 were together in seven of eight sorts, and three sorts wrote exactly the same label, “The Savior.” But C15 (testify more often of Christ) landed with C01 in only two sorts. It went with C04 (statements of God’s love) instead: those two were together in seven of eight. In three sorts, all by Finnish-born sorters, the pair went into a pile about what is hard to say out loud: Aino’s “Speaking of one’s own faith,” Jukka’s “What we don’t easily say aloud,” and Tuula and Pekka’s “What is hard to say out loud.” In the pair sort this was the argument. Pekka wanted testimony with the Savior: “It’s about Him. It goes with Him.” INVENTED QUOTE Tuula answered: “It’s about Him, and it’s the part we find hard. Put it where the hard things are.” INVENTED QUOTE She won. We read this as three sorts’ worth of evidence for a category, and one sort’s worth of evidence that the category is contested.

5. Half the sorts built a pile on who is in the room, or where the class is. Four of eight did. Maricel split her cards by the children’s ages. Liisa split hers by place: home, chapel, video link. Jukka set two cards aside as “Not in our class,” and Samuel made a pile of one “For the Primary teachers.” C10 (a positive message to a parent) shows why this matters. All three teachers of children or youth kept it close: two filed it with the people they teach (S3, S4), and one filed it at home, where “the parent I’d be messaging is me” (S5). Two of the five teachers of adults set it aside as not for their class (S2, S8). Whether a skill applies depends on who you teach.

6. Splitter cards. Six cards landed in six or more different kinds of pile. The two worst, C23 (students share with each other) and C20 (ponder a personal circumstance), landed in seven each. We think we understand C23. For some sorters, sharing with each other is inclusion (Aino); for others it is exactly the thing that is hard to do out loud (Jukka, the pair). The card asks for both, and sorters split on which half they heard.

7. Vocabulary. The labels are plain and understated. “Before Sunday,” “The Savior,” and “Knowing the people” are about as expressive as the study gets. Professional vocabulary appeared in two sorts, both by trained teachers: Aino’s “Wait time” and “Inclusion,” and Oksana’s “New material” and “Checking understanding.” Neither of them disowned it.

Isolated incidents

  • Liisa’s “On the video link” pile holds exactly the cards that still work through a screen: questions, listening, waiting (C11, C12, C14, C16, C21). The picture card went to the chapel. It is one sort, and it belongs to a facet, not to a category.
  • Oksana’s is the only sort organized by time in the lesson. She told us that is how she was trained to plan a lesson, not how she goes looking for help with one.
  • Eero’s sort came closest to the manual’s own sections. “Teaching by the Spirit” is three of its four cards from the Spirit section, and “Invitations and following up” is the only pile in the study with three cards from Invite Diligent Learning — the section that dissolved everywhere else. He got to both by way of his mission, not the manual.
  • Nobody made a pile about keeping the class’s attention, not even the two Primary teachers. Maricel’s worry is the opposite of attention: “six children listening, and I don’t know what they’re thinking.” INVENTED QUOTE
  • Two instrument problems, not findings: Pekka read our Finnish loanword for speculation (C19) as a word about money, and Oksana needed a translation app for C14 and C19. Both go on the list of fixes to the deck.

5. What we’re ignoring — for now, maybe forever

  • Explanations by nationality. Nothing in this report is explained with “Finns are quiet.” Three Finnish-born sorters named the asking cluster after silence and two didn’t, and the fastest, most talkative sort in the study was Finnish-born (Eero). Five sorts are not a nation. Trend 2 goes to the tree test as a question about labels.
  • A lesson-moment facet. One sort (Oksana’s) organized by the parts of a lesson, and she said herself that it is how she plans, not how she looks for help. Parked; the tree test can check whether anyone looks by moment.
  • “Keeping their attention.” The Utah team’s study has this category. Nobody here made the pile, including both Primary teachers. We are not adding a category nobody sorted into just because the other study has one.
  • A category for home. Liisa’s “At home, with my own children” is real and important for families who live hours from their branch. But it is one sort, and the at home value of the where facet covers it.
  • Pekka’s side of the argument. He may be right that testimony belongs with the Savior. C15 carries both categories as attributes, and the tree test will show where people actually look for it.
  • The deck’s Finnish. C19’s loanword and two other handles need better Finnish before the next study. That is a fix to the instrument, not a finding.

Two losses here, and the second one hurts more. Your biggest session is recorded only in your notes, and a record that exists only in your own memory always agrees with you. That fails the appendix requirement: “raw data incomplete or hard to connect to the analysis.” Worse, you “fixed up” the labels. 3. Analysis

Most people made groups about Jesus, about love, and about preparing. Aino made a group called “wait time,” which is a teaching term. Maricel said Finnish kids are very quiet. The group mostly agreed on everything.

Overall the main takeaway is that Finnish people are naturally quiet and reserved, so discussion isn’t really their style and they are more comfortable with the teacher presenting the lesson. The international members were more comfortable with discussion and sharing testimonies, which makes sense culturally.

Grader · Results & Analysis · 7/20 — Beginning

Your first paragraph is a recap, and a thin one. Your second paragraph is where this drops below the over-claiming cap into “speculation disconnected from what participants did and said.” Look at what your own participants did. Aino, a Finnish-born teacher with thirty-seven years in Finnish classrooms, built a pile called “wait time” out of the discussion cards, and one of those cards is literally “resist the tendency to respond to every comment and question and invite the class to respond.” That is a card about not presenting. “Maricel said Finnish kids are quiet” is one comment about six children. “The international members were more comfortable” is two people, and you never tell me what either of them did.

Then there is the sentence itself. A claim about a whole nation built from three solo sorts and one negotiated one isn’t a finding; it’s a stereotype with a sample size. Finnish communication researchers have spent the decades since “The Silent Finn” was published arguing about how much of it is myth. When you catch yourself explaining data with a nationality, stop and ask what the people in front of you actually did.

Grader · Design Decisions · 8/25 — Beginning

This is the most common failure in the assignment, in its purest form. The plan existed before your study ran, and it wasn’t even yours. Another team’s categories come from another team’s participants. Adopting them is a decision about Utah, not a finding about Finland. That is the Beginning band: “decisions ignore/contradict the data.” The rest makes it worse. Machine-translating the labels throws away the one thing your participants gave you in their own words. “Keeping their attention” comes from nothing in your report. And moving discussion to the bottom of the menu turns the stereotype from element 3 into software that real Finnish teachers would have to use. The fix is a sentence shape: “Because [result from our sorts], we will [decision].” If you can’t fill in the because from your own data, the decision isn’t ready.

The clusters travelled; the labels didn’t

The groupings that come from the content held up in both places. The asking cluster held (six sorts in Utah, all eight here), and so did the Savior pair and the preparation cards. What changed was what people called them. The Utah label for the asking cluster, “Getting people to talk,” was used by no Finnish-born sorter here. Three of the five Finnish-born sorts named it after waiting, listening, or silence; the other two named it after questions and after a video link. The music-and-ponder pair (C13 + C20) still shares a pile half the time, but its meaning moved. In Utah the pile was about attention or openers in five of six sorts. Here it is stillness in two of four, an opener in one, and “for the little ones” in the last. Clusters describe the content. Labels describe the people. That is why a card sort’s labels have to be harvested again in every population you design for, while its clusters might just transfer.

The category a population doesn’t make can matter most

The Timpanogos report’s riskiest call was to leave out a Spirit category, because only one sort in eight made one. Here four of eight did, built around prayer and sacred music, and two of the four never used the word Spirit. The attention pile went the other way, from three sorts to none. The people pile kept its place but changed its centre. In Utah it was built on what you do for people during the week (praying for them and messaging a parent together in six sorts). Here it is built on who they are in the room (their interests and being needed, together in seven), and the praying-and-messaging pair survived in only one sort. Neither report was wrong. They studied different people, and each was right to follow its own data rather than the other’s.

This is what single source, multiple views is for

Put the two Report As side by side and look at what would have to change in a real build. Not one skill changes. The Utah view files the blocks into five categories and gives each one a lesson-moment attribute. The Finland view files the same blocks into six categories, adds two attributes (who is in the room, and where), and carries labels harvested in two languages. The course’s through-line says to design a data scheme of blocks with attributes and to make presentation a matter of selecting on them, because attributes will be added and modified. When the population changed, that is exactly what happened. A team that had hard-coded Utah’s categories into its pages, as Report B proposes, would have to rebuild. A team with a data scheme adds two attributes and a view. The companion’s single-source, multiple-views tutorial walks through that pattern on the real build.
