The Northern Finland stake version

Everything else in this companion happens in one place, the Timpanogos Shadows Ward in North Orem, Utah. This section asks what would happen if you ran the same study somewhere else. It keeps the same assignment, the same method, the same rubric, and the same 24 cards, card for card. Only the people change: invented members of the Jyväskylä Finland Stake (YEW-vass-kew-la), the stake that covers the northern and eastern two-thirds of Finland, from the lakes of central Finland to Rovaniemi on the Arctic Circle.

SPECULATION In the version of it we imagined, a class can be three people, a laptop, and two languages. Some of the teachers teach for a living, with master’s degrees, and some are learning Finnish as they go.

The question this section exists to put in front of you is the one every card-sort report should ask itself: how much of what we found belongs to the people we asked?

Read this first: this version is more speculative than the rest

The stake is real; the people are not. The Jyväskylä Finland Stake was organized in 2021. The twelve members on these pages were made up by AI for this course, belong to no real congregation, and are based on nobody. No stake statistics were invented, and no invented person holds a leadership calling in a real unit.

The findings are researched guesses, not the instructor’s convictions. The Timpanogos studies encode what the instructor believes about teachers he knows. This version was built from published sources — Church statistics, Finnish education and census figures, research on Finnish communication — and no Finnish Latter-day Saint was asked. Every pattern here is a hypothesis a real study could test.

What moved, in one screen

FICTITIOUS DATA Invented results from two invented studies, counted out of eight sorts each. The full side-by-side is computed from both studies’ pile data.

  • The clusters travelled.

    The discussion cards stayed together in both places (six sorts in Utah, all eight here), and so did the Savior pair and the preparation cards. Groupings that come from the content seem to transfer.

  • The labels didn’t.

    In Utah the discussion pile was called “Getting people to talk.” No Finnish-born sorter here used a label like that; three of the five named it after waiting, listening, or silence. The winning label, “Asking and waiting,” was picked because it is the one both halves could read as theirs.

  • The Spirit came back — as quiet.

    The Utah report’s riskiest call was leaving out a Spirit category (one sort in eight made one). Here four of eight did, with prayer and sacred music at the centre, and two of the four never used the word Spirit.

  • Testimony moved.

    In Utah, “testify more often of Christ” sat with the Savior in seven sorts; here it did in two. Three sorts put it in a pile about what is hard to say out loud.

  • The room became a facet.

    Half the sorts built a pile on who is in the room or where the class happens: the children’s ages, the chapel, the video link, home. No Utah sort built one, though the Utah report noticed an age-of-audience hint in three Primary sorts and deferred it.

One data scheme can serve both stakes. The skills don’t change. The Finland view adds two attributes and harvests its labels in two languages, and the pages keep selecting on attributes. That is the course’s through-line, playing out on a real change of population.
