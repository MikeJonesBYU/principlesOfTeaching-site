Wireframe — Team Report

CS 356 · Project 1 · Principles of Teaching redesign · November 2026

1. The wireframe

Link: Team A wireframe — prototypes/wireframe/team-a/. Black and white, one font, generic boxes, no images. It runs from a single data file, wireframe-data.js: one record per information block, 53 of them, each carrying a category, a group, and a moment list. No skill is written into any page. Every screen is produced by selecting on those attributes, so all 53 blocks are filed and none of them is filed twice.

2. Design rationale

The five categories are the card sort’s. Four of them each came out of six of eight sorts. “Keeping their attention” came out of three, all Primary teachers, and half this ward teaches children, so we kept it.

The labels are participants’ words. L1 and L2 use their sticky-note wording — “Not answering it yourself” — because our newest teacher could not parse 7 of the 24 official skill names. Verbatim manual wording appears only at L3, linked to its manual page.

There is deliberately no Spirit category. One sort in eight made one. Our card-sort report called it our riskiest call and named what would reopen it — people looking for what they do not sort by — which is what T09 · Is it the Spirit? tests.

The second view is an attribute, not a sixth box. Our sort produced one fully sequential sorter, an “Openers” pile, and the same two cards together in six of eight sorts while the pile’s meaning changed around them: people asking two questions at once, what problem is this and when in the lesson. So moment is a value on every block, not a category; the by-moment view renders from it alone. All 53 blocks are filed.

The clusters that never separated stayed together: the four discussion cards in one category, the two Christ cards that co-occurred in 8 of 8 sorts in one group, praying for a student and messaging a parent both under “Caring about them during the week.”

Logging. Every click is logged with its page and route; the log exports as JSON or CSV. Bonus test mode: ten scenarios shuffled per run, timed to a tenth of a second, surviving a reload, with an “I would give up” button. Our tree test (the next turn-in) will run inside it.

Grader · 1. Met the functional spec · 18/18 — Skilled

I opened this and went looking for the seams. There aren’t any.

  • Every required behavior works. The category view is three levels deep and the moment view two; all 53 blocks are filed; every path ends on a real leaf with a working link out to the manual page it quotes; the click log exports in both formats and the exports actually parse. “The prototype meets every functional requirement in the assignment spec, and the functionality works reliably: a user can exercise each required behavior without workarounds, errors, or coaching.”
  • The bonus went past the assignment. Ten scenarios, shuffled per run, timed, persisted across a reload.
  • The give-up button is the tell. Most teams treat abandonment as missing data; you made it a recordable outcome.
  • So is the interrupted-run handling. Reload mid-session and it offers to resume at the situation you were on, instead of silently starting over and quietly contaminating your timings. Those two details are the difference between building a feature and building an instrument.

Bonus credit awarded — and then you turned the thing on yourselves and used it to run your tree test, which is the best argument for the bonus I have seen.

Grader · 2. Met the visual design spec · 13/13 — Skilled

True wireframe fidelity, held all the way down — and the layout is still doing work.

  • The discipline holds. One typeface, no color, no imagery, boxes that are obviously boxes.
  • The three-zone structure repeats. Where you are, what is inside it, where you can go — on every screen at every level, so depth is legible without a single decorative cue.
  • The L3 leaf reads as an end state, not as another list.

“At wireframe fidelity, layout and visual hierarchy do the work and clearly support the user’s goals.” That is this band, exactly.

Grader · 3. Met the information architecture spec · 13/13 — Skilled

Both of your views are real ways in, not one real one and one alibi.

  • Two questions, two doors. The category view answers “what problem am I having,” the moment view answers “where am I in the lesson,” and each is a sensible way to look for this content. “There are multiple reasonable ways to find things, and each way makes sense for the content and the users. Categories hold together; the hierarchy reflects deliberate design decisions.”
  • You did not decide where each skill really goes. Let me name that out loud for everyone reading, because it is the architectural decision this course is actually about: you gave every block a set of attributes and made each page a selection over them.
  • That is why the moment view cost you nothing structurally — and why the next attribute will cost you nothing either.

Top band, without an argument.

Grader · 4. Grounded in the previous study · 16/16 — Skilled

I checked your rationale against the artifact line by line, and “the words and the artifact agree.”

  • Labels from participants’ own wording — they are, and they are the same strings your card-sort standardization table harvested.
  • Verbatim manual names only at L3, because your newest teacher couldn’t parse them — that is exactly what the artifact does.
  • The co-occurrence clusters stayed together — they did.
  • You left out the Spirit category. The hardest move to defend in a meeting: your data didn’t support one, you said out loud that this was your riskiest call, and then you built the test for it into the prototype as T09 · Is it the Spirit?

“The information architecture is not just reasonable; it is demonstrably grounded in the needs of your users.” Full marks.

Wireframe

CS 356

Our wireframe

Our wireframe is here: Team B wireframe. It has one page for every information block from our card sort, and two ways to browse: our seven categories, and the manual’s five sections. There are 21 block pages. Three of the cards from our card sort don’t have pages because we took those cards out during the sort and they never came back, so there was nothing to make a page for.

Rationale. Based on our card sort we went with seven categories: Teaching Fundamentals, Spiritual Teaching, Engagement, Questions, Preparation, Love, and Misc. We feel like our seven categories cover everything and match the manual while still being about problems, which is what people said they wanted. Misc is for the ones that didn’t really fit anywhere, which is only three of them so it’s not that bad. For the second categorization we used the manual’s five sections since that’s an organization people already know, and we figure most people will use the categories anyway.

We also added a click log like the spec says. There’s a counter on every page showing how many clicks happened, and an export button on the home page that dumps the log into a text box so you can copy it out. We didn’t get to the bonus test mode. Everything is black and white with boxes and placeholder text where the content would go.

Grader · 1. Met the functional spec · 9/18 — Beginning

Start with what works, because it does: seven category pages, five section pages, 21 block pages, every link resolves, and a user can click from the home screen to a labeled end state without anything breaking. That is a real artifact and you built it by hand.

Now the three gaps, and they are load-bearing ones. Coverage. The spec asks for every information block from your card sort. Three of them — C03, C18, C19 — have no page at all, in either view. I understand why; that is your card sort’s mid-study deck change following you into the next assignment, which is worth noticing on its own. The block pages dead-end. They have exactly one way out — a single “< Back to [category]” link — so a user who arrived from a section page is silently handed off into the other categorization and cannot get back to where they were. Every route in should have a route back to where it came from. The click log. clickLog is a plain array that is created fresh on every page load, and the export button exists only on the home page. So the log records clicks on one page and then discards them the moment the user navigates, which means it can never record the one thing a click log is for: a path. The counter on a block page reads 0 no matter how many clicks it took the user to get there.

“Significant required functionality is missing or does not work; the prototype demonstrates the idea but cannot deliver the behaviors the spec requires.” That is this band. The fix for the log is small and worth doing before v1: write the array to sessionStorage on every click and read it back on load, and put the export control on every page. That one change turns a counter into an instrument — and it is the same change that would have given your tree test the paths it does not have.

Grader · 2. Met the visual design spec · 10/13 — Competent

You held the hard part of wireframe fidelity: no images, no decoration, placeholder text where content goes, boxes that look like boxes. Nobody is going to mistake this for a finished design, which is the point.

What keeps it out of the top band is “choices made without full intention.” The spec says one font; your stylesheet sets Arial for body text and Georgia for every heading. The spec says black and white; you never declared a link color, so every link on the site renders in the browser’s default blue and turns purple once visited — which means color is in your wireframe, carrying meaning you did not choose. Three box styles (solid 1px, solid 2px, dotted) wrap content of the same kind, so the border weight implies a hierarchy that isn’t there. And the page margins are set per page type — four different values — so the content block visibly jumps as a user clicks down a level; the h1 is centered on the home page and on two of your seven category pages (Preparation and Questions) and left-aligned everywhere else, which is the same inconsistency in a second place. None of these is a disaster and all of them are a minute’s work: one font family, a { color: #000 }, one box class, one margin. Fidelity is a constraint you hold on purpose, not a look you approximate.

Grader · 3. Met the IA design spec · 7/13 — Beginning

“The information architecture looks improvised rather than designed: a single path to content, or categories that do not hold together.” Two categories in particular do not hold. Misc is a decision not to decide; it holds pictures and videos of Christ, sacred music, and an opening invitation tied to the lesson outcome, which are three things with an obvious shared property — they are all things you do at the start of a lesson — sitting in a bin named for the absence of a property. Preparation holds “ask about students’ interests” and “pray for your students by name” next to “search the scriptures for depth,” which reads as “things that happen before Sunday” on Monday and “things about loving people” on Tuesday. Your own tree-test data, which you collected and did not read this way, says the same thing: every single first click that landed on Misc was on a task Misc could not answer.

You do have a second path, so this is not the “single path” half of the band — but it does not rescue you. Your own card-sort analysis concluded that nobody understands the manual’s organization, which makes shipping it as a way in a decision your own evidence argues against. (That two blocks sit in the category view and not in this one is not the problem — a second view does not have to reach everything, so long as every block is reachable from one view or another.) The rubric wants alternate paths that genuinely help users find things. Ask of any second view: what question does this one answer that the first one doesn’t?

Grader · 4. Grounded in the previous study · 8/16 — Beginning

“The rationale gestures at the previous study without specific findings, or the prototype’s design shows no visible relationship to what the study found.” Read your rationale again and count the findings it cites: none. Not one number, not one participant, not one pile. “Based on our card sort” is the whole citation, and it is followed by a category list your card sort did not produce.

Two of those seven names are worse than uncited, and I flagged this on your card sort so I want to be concrete. You showed your sorters “Questions” and “Preparing” as starter examples before an open sort. Those two categories are now two of your seven, and they are the two your tree-test participants found things in most reliably — because your own tasks name them. That is a loop closing on itself: you handed people the words, they handed them back, and the wireframe treats that as a finding. And “match the manual while still being about problems” is the same sentence your card-sort analysis contradicted one page earlier when it said nobody understands the manual’s organization.

The repair is mechanical and I would take it in this order. For every category, write “Because [result from the card sort], we will [this category].” The ones where you can’t fill in the because are the ones to cut. You will find you have four or five, not seven, and the ones that survive will be defensible to anyone.

What separates 60 from 34

Both teams were handed the same 53 skills and the same assignment, and both came back with something you can click through. The 26 points between them are almost entirely about where the structure lives.

Team A put it in one file. Every skill is a block with a category, a group, and a lesson-moment attribute, and every page — both hierarchies, the block pages, the test mode’s answer key — is rendered by selecting on those attributes. Nothing is hand-placed anywhere, so nothing can be missing from one view and present in another, and the outline of the whole site can be generated from the data and checked against the artifact by a stranger. That is what “facets and values, decoupled from display” buys at wireframe fidelity, and it is why the functional and IA criteria could pay full marks: the alternate path genuinely helps because it is the same content seen through a different attribute, not a second set of pages that happens to overlap the first.

Team B put it in 34 pages. Each page was typed by hand, which is why three of their 24 cards never came back after the card sort pulled them, why the manual-section view silently lacks two blocks the category view has, why the click log starts over on every page, and why “Misc” exists at all — a hand-built hierarchy needs somewhere to put the things that did not fit, and a data-driven one does not. None of that is carelessness. It is the predictable cost of the approach, and the grade prices the approach, not the effort: functional at Beginning because required behaviors are absent, IA at Beginning because the categories do not hold together, grounded at Beginning because two of the seven categories are the card sort’s own priming echo rather than a finding. The visual criterion is the one place the two teams are close, and even there the gap is intention, not taste: two fonts and default link blue are what a wireframe looks like when nobody decided.

The grade that matters most is the one this page does not give. A wireframe is an instrument for the study that comes next. Team A’s give-up button, randomized task order, persisted runs, and CSV export are why their tree test, over on the next page, can be re-derived by anyone; Team B’s page-local click counter is why theirs cannot. Turn-in 2 and Turn-in 3 are graded separately, on different rubrics, but the second one is largely decided by the first. Build the instrument before you need the answer.

In this chain, Team A’s wireframe is binding. It is the artifact the tree test ran on, and its data scheme — five categories, fifteen groups in harvested language, a lesson-moment attribute on every block — is what prototype v1 inherits and revises. Both wireframes are frozen as of the tree test, so every citation above stays checkable.
