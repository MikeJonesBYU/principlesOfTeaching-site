What to put on the cards?

TL;DR

  • It depends on what your target users already know about the topic. Basically, IYKYK.
  • If you do need to add something to a card, make it one short sentence.
  • If you need more than a sentence, your combination of population and topic is probably not a great fit.

Example: a card sort of BYU CS classes

And does it make sense to run a card sort on CS classes with fifth graders at all?

This question came up a lot in class. The short answer is as little as possible: the name of the information item, and usually nothing else. The longer answer doesn’t start with the cards. It starts with the question your study is trying to answer, and it ends with who you picked to do the sorting.

What’s real on this page. The advice is the instructor’s own, organized from class notes, and every source is real and listed under Works cited. The albums, restaurants, D&D races, learning activities, and course numbers are illustrations. The only invented material is from our example card sort, whose participants are members of the companion’s made-up ward. It’s stamped FICTITIOUS DATA wherever it appears. Start with the question your study answers

In any study design, the first thing to ask is: what question am I trying to answer with this study? Everything else flows from that. For the open card sort in this project, the question is:

How do people in my narrow population organize these blocks of information, and what do they call the groups?

You give people cards so you can see how they organize them, and you want to see that so you can build a website for them. That one sentence ties the whole chain together:

  1. Start

    Your question

    How does this population organize these items, and what do they call the groups?

  2. Decides

    Who sorts

    A narrow population you picked, who know these items.

  3. Decides

    What’s on the cards

    Whatever lets them recognize each item, and nothing more.

  4. Produces

    The website

    Organized the way they organized the cards, for the people who sorted them.

This is a user-driven information architecture for a narrow population that you picked. The cards exist to show you how that population organizes things. They aren’t there to show your own organization, the source’s, or whatever the cards happen to say.

The short answer: the name, and that’s about it

The card has to carry the name of the information item. After that, you want as little as possible, because you’re trying to see how people organize the items themselves.

“If you give them much else, they’re going to organize things based on whatever else is on the card.”

From class, lightly edited

The rule

Put the name of the information item on the card.

If you have to, add one very short sentence saying what it is.

Nothing else.

Whatever you put on the card, you get back

Say you’re building a site for a record collection, and you print each card with the album’s name and the year it came out. Here’s what happens next:

That’s why it matters that the card holds just the label. The label is what people actually know about the item, and it’s what they’ll have in mind when they come to your site looking for it.

“Whatever that label brings to mind for the person doing the card sort is exactly what you want to organize your website on. And if the card doesn’t bring anything to mind, they’re not the right person to be in the study.”

What others have found

Extra words steer the cards people are unsure about, even when they don’t notice the words. Optimal Workshop’s card-description test ran the same 30-card sort four times, with 30 new people each round. Only the card changed:

Two things happened at once:

Where & what to study

Clear labels didn’t move. Cards like this one landed with the same neighbors in every round, with or without extra words.

Life experiences

Ambiguous labels did. In round 2, cards like this one were grouped with the rest of their current site section much more often than in the other rounds.

Said they noticed the descriptions ~40%

And most people said they hadn’t noticed the descriptions at all. So the lesson isn’t that people ignore what’s on the card. It’s the opposite. The extra words did their work on exactly the cards people were unsure about, which are the cards you most want to learn about, and the participants couldn’t have told you it happened.

  • Sorting on the surface is a known weakness. Spencer and Warfel’s guide warns that participants “may just sort it by surface characteristics such as document types.” Every field you print on a card gives them another surface to sort on.
  • Shared words get piled together. Nielsen Norman Group’s article on keyword matching finds that participants often group cards that share a word instead of thinking about what each card means, because matching words takes less effort.

When the name isn’t enough: two more questions

So the name, and that’s it, unless you have to do more. To know whether you have to do more, answer two more questions.

Who’s in your narrow population?

We scoped this study to a narrow population on purpose, because that simplifies a lot. Later in your career, and later in this course, you’ll design for diverse populations with many kinds of people in them. Not yet.

What do they know about the things you’re sorting?

A lot? Nothing? Somewhere in between? Once you know your population and what they know about these items, you can decide what goes on the card.

When they don’t know what the cards mean

If your participants really don’t know anything about what’s on the cards, they have nothing meaningful to sort on. They may fall back on alphabetical order, or on something else that has nothing to do with what the cards mean, like which words look alike. That isn’t interesting, and it won’t help you build a site. A card sort needs a population that knows something and cards that tell that population something.

Same activities, two populations

Take learning activities for neurodivergent learners. If your population is teachers, you can assume a fair amount about what they know about each activity. If it’s parents who are new to all of this, you can’t. Whoever the population is, though, you’re answering the same question: how does this population organize these things? Participants have to understand each item well enough to organize it. They don’t have to understand it like an expert.

So if you sort with parents, don’t turn each card into a lesson on a teaching method so that parents can sort the way an experienced teacher would. A study with parents answers how parents organize these things. They need to understand each one just well enough to sort by what the items mean.

Dungeons & Dragons races

For intermediate or advanced players, just put the race’s name on the card. They know what you’re talking about, and you want to see how they organize the races so you can build a site for them. For someone who has never played, a card that says “Tiefling” communicates nothing, and they won’t know how to sort it. It’s not even clear why you’d build a site to help them find a race. The first thing they need is to learn what the races are and what they mean.

Restaurants

We have two restaurant projects, and restaurants complicate this a little. Can your population look at a restaurant’s name and know something about it? For people who live in Provo and the restaurants in Provo, that’s a reasonable assumption. For restaurants in Finland, none of us would be much use. So pick the people who know the restaurants, give them the names, and let them bring what they know. If you put price, cuisine, and hours on every card, you change how they organize. That change may not show up in how they actually look for a restaurant on your site later. It’s the album-year problem again.

What others have found

  • What people know changes what they sort on. In Chi and colleagues’ physics-sorting study, novices grouped physics problems by what was on the surface, such as the objects in each problem. Experts grouped them by the principle that solves them.
  • The method assumes participants know the content. In Spencer and Warfel’s guide, “participants understand most of the content” is the easy case for card sorting and “complex or specialist content” is the challenging one. They also say the most important thing about participants is that they are representative of your user group.

If you add a sentence, keep it small

If you need to, you can add a very short sentence to a card that clarifies what you mean. Put as little information in that sentence as you can, so people don’t sort on some attribute of it. Ideally, you put just the name on the card and see what people do with it, and you’ve picked a population that knows what to do with a name.

Three ways to write the sentence for parents who are new to this:

When you do write one:

  • Say what the thing is. Don’t say where it lives now. Leave its specs (price, year, setup time, age range) off too, unless how people handle those specs is what you’re studying.
  • Use your population’s words, not the words from the source you got the items from.
  • Don’t repeat words across cards. Cards that share a word get piled together because of the word.
  • A quick test: would someone in your population say this sentence when telling a friend what the thing is? If not, it’s a data field, not a description.

What others advise

Optimal Workshop’s test ends with the same advice: use descriptions only when “it’s not possible or practical” to translate complex or technical labels into plain language, and write them without repeated words or hints about an existing structure.

Why this never came up before

In earlier versions of this course, the first information architecture project was organizing BYU computer science classes. Everyone in CS 356 was a BYU CS student, and they ran their card sorts with other BYU CS students. When a card said “CS 252,” everybody knew what it meant, and nobody had to explain anything on the card.

CS 252

“Oh, I took that.”

A BYU CS student: knows exactly where it goes.

“What’s a CS?”

A fifth grader: nothing to sort on.

Now you pick your own topic and your own population, so that assumption has to be made explicit: the people in your study should know what you mean by the name on the card. Run a card sort of BYU CS course names with fifth graders at an elementary school and it doesn’t make sense. You can’t put “CS 252” on a card and expect them to have any idea what you mean.

So the goal is to pick a topic and a population together, so that you can put an item’s name on a card and the people in your population know a good deal about that thing. If you’re doing D&D races, your population is people who can look at a race’s name and have a reasonable, shared understanding of what it means. If you’d have to copy a race’s whole record onto the card, you probably need a different population.

In our example card sort

The companion’s card sort ran into both halves of this page. Its population is people who teach in one ward. Its items are skills from the Teacher Development manual. Each of its 24 cards is one skill, quoted word for word from the manual as the card’s name, with nothing added (see the deck). For most participants that was enough. They teach, and the skills describe things teachers do.

What this means for your project this semester

You’ve already pitched your topic and population. Treat this as strong advice, not a new rule. Before you print the deck, run a quick check:

Hand a few name-only cards to someone like your participants. Can they tell you what each one is? Try it on someone who won’t be in the study.

Yes, all of them

Name only.

You’re done. Print the deck.

Most, not all

Add one short sentence to those cards.

Keep it to what the thing is. Say in your report which cards got one and what it said.

Mostly no

That’s a warning sign about your population.

The fix is usually the people, not the card. Don’t copy the record onto the card to compensate.

  1. Put the name on every card, in the words your population would use.
  2. You don’t have to change your topic or population now. If some cards carry a sentence, your report should say which cards, what each sentence said, and why.
  3. Check your piles for echoes. A group named with a word from your own sentences is suspect.

Next year the course will lean further in this direction: pick the topic and the population together, so that the name alone is enough.

Alternative views

Not everyone who writes about card sorting puts it as bluntly as this page does. These are the main places where others see it differently, and how each one fits with the advice above.

Interactive digital courses for skill enhancements

“Give the label enough detail to be understood.”

Spencer and Warfel’s guide says labels should be short enough to read quickly “yet detailed enough that participants can understand what the content is,” with an optional short description or image on the back of the card. Nielsen Norman Group’s keyword-matching article goes further. To break up keyword matching, it suggests rewriting short labels into fuller descriptions, like the pair shown here.

How it fits: everyone agrees that participants have to understand the item. The disagreement is about where that understanding comes from. This page puts it in the population, and these sources put more of it on the card. If you do add words, remember round 2 of Optimal Workshop’s test: every word you add can steer the sort.

“Sort with your real users, even if they’re beginners.”

If a site is for newcomers, a structure built from experts’ sorts may fail them, because experts and novices group things differently (Chi and colleagues’ physics-sorting study again). On this view, a novice sort is valuable because it shows how novices think, and a site for novices should be built on that.

How it fits: that’s a fair point, and it applies when your site’s real audience is beginners. But the problem above still holds. People who know nothing about the items have little to sort on but the words themselves, and what they need first is teaching, not help finding things. There are middle routes. One is to give beginners the categories and have them place the cards (a closed sort, sketched here). Robertson and colleagues’ novice study found that novices’ results came much closer to experts’ in a closed sort than in an open one. Another is to build the structure some other way and tree-test it with beginners.

Staff Policies
Find holiday entitlement

“Put tasks on the cards, not things.”

Hudson’s encyclopedia chapter recommends starting from user goals rather than page names, as in the pair shown here, because a tidy set of pages can still fail people who are trying to get something done. Katsanos and colleagues’ task-based card sort proposes a version of open card sorting built on tasks.

How it fits: this is a different answer to “what is the information item?” rather than a different answer to “what goes on the card?” If your users arrive with tasks, a task card can still be just a name: the task, in their words.

“Use pictures, or the things themselves.”

Hudson’s chapter suggests that in unfamiliar domains, or where language is the barrier, participants can sort photographs or even the objects themselves. Rugg and McGeorge’s tutorial on sorting describes picture sorts and item sorts alongside card sorts.

How it fits: this is useful when words are the problem, for example sensory tools like the wobble cushion sketched here, which parents would recognize on sight but not by name. But a picture carries a lot of information. Color, size, and brand are all things people can sort on, so the “as little as possible” rule applies to the picture too.

“Put the attributes on the cards, on purpose.”

If what you want to know is which attributes matter rather than how people group the items, the attributes are the point. In a repeated single-criterion sort (Rugg and McGeorge’s tutorial), participants sort the same deck several times, each time by one criterion they choose and name. Or you can put the attributes themselves on cards and have people rank them, as sketched here. That ranking tells you which filters to build.

How it fits: this is this page’s first principle. Start with the question: a different question calls for different cards. Just don’t run it as your open card sort.
